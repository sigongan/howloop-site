You take a roast out of the oven at exactly the temperature the recipe asked for, rest it, slice it — and it's overcooked. Nothing went wrong with your thermometer. The roast kept cooking after it left the oven. This is **carryover cooking**, and once you plan for it, you'll hit your target far more often.

## The physics in one paragraph

Heat moves from hot to cold. When food cooks, its outside gets much hotter than its centre — a roast in a 200°C oven might have a surface well above 100°C while the middle is still in the 50s. When you take it out, that hot outer layer doesn't stop being hot. Heat continues to flow inwards, towards the cooler centre, while the surface also loses heat to the air. For a while, the centre keeps warming up — until the temperature evens out across the food, and then the whole thing slowly cools.

## How big is the rise?

Carryover depends on three main things:

**1. Size and thickness.** A large roast holds a big reservoir of heat in its outer layers and takes a long time to even out, so its centre can climb a long way. A thin steak or fish fillet has little stored heat and cools quickly, so the rise is small.

**2. Cooking temperature.** The hotter the oven, grill or pan, the steeper the temperature difference between surface and centre, and the more heat there is to move inwards. Meat roasted at 220°C shows far more carryover than the same cut roasted at 120°C.

**3. Where it rests.** Food rested somewhere warm, covered tightly with foil or left in a hot pan keeps more heat and climbs further. Food rested on a rack in open air rises less.

As rough guides:

| Food | Typical carryover rise |
|---|---|
| Thin steak, chop or fish fillet (under 2 cm) | 1–2°C |
| Thick steak (3–5 cm), seared hot | 3–5°C |
| Whole chicken roasted hot | 3–6°C |
| Large beef or lamb roast, high oven | 5–10°C |
| Anything cooked gently (low oven, reverse sear, sous vide) | Little to none |

These are estimates. The only way to know for your oven and your cuts is to leave a probe thermometer in during resting and watch what happens.

## Planning pull temperatures

Most cooks decide on the **final temperature** they want and remove the food a few degrees earlier, letting carryover finish the job. A guide for doneness in red meat, as commonly used in restaurant kitchens:

| Doneness (beef, lamb) | Final internal temperature | Pull a thick steak at about | Pull a large roast at about |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rare | 50–52°C | 47°C | 45°C |
| Medium-rare | 54–57°C | 51–52°C | 49–50°C |
| Medium | 60–63°C | 57°C | 55°C |
| Medium-well | 65–68°C | 62°C | 60°C |

:::safety Food-safety note
These doneness temperatures are culinary conventions, not safety targets. US Department of Agriculture consumer guidance is to cook whole cuts of beef, lamb, pork and veal to at least 63°C (145°F) and rest them for three minutes; to cook minced (ground) meats to 71°C; and to cook all poultry to 74°C (165°F). Minced meat and poultry should always be cooked through, and people at higher risk of foodborne illness should follow the official temperatures.
:::

## It's not just meat

**Fish** has a narrow window between moist and dry, so carryover matters a lot. Pull fillets just before they reach your target, when the centre is still slightly translucent.

**Eggs** keep cooking in the pan. Scrambled eggs should come off the heat while they still look slightly wet; by the time they reach the plate they'll be set. (See [what temperature eggs cook at](/what-temperature-do-eggs-cook/).)

**Custards and baked cheesecakes** are pulled from the oven with a slight wobble in the centre, which sets as they cool.

**Vegetables** carry on softening after draining, which is why blanched vegetables are plunged into iced water if you want to keep them crisp (see [why green vegetables turn grey](/why-green-vegetables-turn-grey/)).

## How to reduce or control carryover

- **Cook gently.** A lower oven temperature, or reverse searing (cooking low first, searing at the end), keeps the temperature difference inside the food small, so there's little carryover to manage.
- **Rest on a rack, uncovered.** This lets the surface lose heat and keeps the crust crisp. Tent loosely with foil only if the meat will rest for a long time and you need to keep it warm.
- **Move food out of hot pans.** A cast-iron pan stays hot long after you turn it off; leaving food in it keeps cooking it from below.
- **Measure in the right place.** Insert the probe into the thickest part, away from bone, and check more than one spot in a large roast.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Experienced cooks know their carryover by heart for the cuts they cook every night. On a grill section, steaks come off a few degrees under the order and rest while the plate is built; large joints for a carvery are pulled well under target and rested for 20–30 minutes, with a probe left in. It's one of the habits that separates consistent kitchens from lucky ones.
:::

## Common questions

### Should I cover resting meat with foil?

Only loosely, and only if it will rest for a long time and you need to keep it warm. Wrapping tightly traps steam, which softens the crust, and holds in heat, which increases carryover. For steaks and short rests, leave meat uncovered on a rack.

### How long should meat rest?

As a guide: thin steaks and chops 2–5 minutes, thick steaks 5–10 minutes, whole chickens 10–15 minutes and large roasts 15–30 minutes. The more useful approach is to watch a probe thermometer: once the centre stops rising, carryover is complete.

### Does carryover affect fish more than meat?

Fish has less connective tissue and a narrower window between moist and dry, so a couple of degrees of carryover is more noticeable. Thin fillets cook so quickly that the safest approach is to remove them from the pan just before they look done.

## The takeaway

Food keeps cooking after the heat is off because its hot outside keeps feeding heat to its cooler centre. The bigger the food and the hotter you cook it, the bigger the rise. Pull early, rest on a rack, use a thermometer, and let carryover finish the cooking for you.
