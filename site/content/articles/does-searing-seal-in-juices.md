Few kitchen ideas are as stubborn as this one: sear meat over high heat first to "seal in the juices", then finish cooking it more gently. It sounds sensible. A browned crust *looks* like a barrier. But it isn't one — and the real reason to sear is better than the myth.

## Where the idea came from

The theory is usually traced to the German chemist **Justus von Liebig**, one of the most influential scientists of the 19th century. In the 1840s and 1850s he argued that exposing meat to intense heat would quickly coagulate the proteins at its surface and trap the juices inside. Liebig's reputation gave the idea enormous authority, and it spread through cookbooks and kitchens.

When cooks and researchers actually tested it — most clearly in experiments in the 1930s — the theory didn't survive. Seared roasts lost as much moisture as unseared ones — and sometimes more, because searing exposes the surface to very high temperatures. Food writer Harold McGee summarises this history in *On Food and Cooking*: the crust that searing creates is not waterproof.

## Listen to the pan

You can test the myth with your ears. A steak in a hot pan sizzles continuously from start to finish. That sizzle is the sound of water hitting hot metal and turning to steam — water coming out of the meat. If the crust sealed the surface, the sizzle would stop once it had formed. It doesn't.

## What really controls juiciness

Meat is about 75% water, much of it held inside and between muscle fibres. As meat heats:

- From around 40–50°C, the main muscle proteins begin to unfold and contract, squeezing some water out of the fibres.
- Between roughly 55 and 65°C, the collagen in the sheaths around muscle fibres shrinks, squeezing the fibres like a wrung towel and pushing out much more liquid.
- Above that, the meat keeps losing moisture, becoming firmer and drier.

So the amount of juice that leaves the meat is governed mainly by **how hot the inside gets**, not by what happens on the surface. A steak cooked to medium-rare stays juicy because its centre never reaches the temperatures where the heaviest squeezing happens. A well-done steak loses much more water, crust or no crust.

## So why sear at all? Flavour

Searing is worth doing for one big reason: the **Maillard reaction**. When the surface of meat dries and goes well above the boiling point of water, sugars and amino acids react to form hundreds of new compounds with roasted, savoury, meaty aromas, along with brown pigments. That is the flavour of a grilled steak, a roast chicken skin or a well-browned stew base. (Read more in [the Maillard reaction vs caramelisation](/maillard-reaction-vs-caramelisation/).)

Searing also creates **texture contrast** — a crisp, slightly crunchy exterior against a tender interior — and it renders surface fat. And because many people perceive browned meat as juicier and richer, a good crust can make meat *taste* juicier, even though it doesn't hold in any extra water.

## Sear first or sear last?

Because searing doesn't seal anything, there is no rule that it has to come first.

**Sear first** (the traditional method) works well for thin cuts that cook through in a few minutes in the pan. For thick cuts, though, it tends to produce a thick grey band of overcooked meat under the crust before the centre is done.

**Sear last** — the **reverse sear** — means cooking a thick steak or roast gently in a low oven (or a water bath) until the inside is a few degrees below your target, then searing it hard for a minute or two per side. Because the meat's surface is already warm and dry, it browns quickly, and the interior stays evenly pink from edge to edge with almost no grey band.

## How to get a better sear

1. **Dry the surface.** Pat meat dry with paper towel. Better still, salt it and leave it uncovered in the fridge for a few hours or overnight; the surface dries out and browns much faster. (See [how brining works](/how-brining-works/) for why salting ahead also seasons the meat more deeply.)
2. **Get the pan properly hot.** Use a heavy pan that holds heat — cast iron or thick stainless steel — and a high-smoke-point oil.
3. **Don't crowd.** Too much meat in the pan drops its temperature and traps steam, so the meat stews instead of browning. Cook in batches if needed.
4. **Leave it alone.** Let the crust form before moving the meat. Once it has browned, it usually releases from the pan by itself.
5. **Use a thermometer for doneness.** The crust tells you nothing about the inside; a probe thermometer does.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Restaurant kitchens commonly cook thick steaks, duck breasts and large roasts partly ahead — in a low oven or a temperature-controlled water bath — and then sear them to order. It gives an even interior and a fast, reliable crust when the docket comes in, and it shows how little the order of operations matters to juiciness.
:::

## Common questions

### Should meat come to room temperature before searing?

It makes less difference than many recipes suggest. A thick steak left out for 20–30 minutes warms only its outer layer by a few degrees; the centre barely changes. Drying the surface matters far more for a good sear. For food safety, keep raw meat out of the fridge for as short a time as practical.

### Does salting meat before searing draw out the juices?

Only briefly. Salt first pulls moisture to the surface, but given about 40 minutes or more, that liquid is drawn back in, seasoning the meat and leaving a drier surface that browns well. Salt either just before cooking or well ahead, not 10–20 minutes before.

### Does flipping a steak often ruin the crust?

Not necessarily. Frequent flipping cooks a steak more evenly and slightly faster because neither side has long to cool. You can still get a good crust if the pan is hot enough — it simply builds up over more turns.

## The takeaway

Searing doesn't seal in juices; the evidence has been clear for nearly a century. It does something more valuable: it creates flavour and texture through browning. Control juiciness with internal temperature, and sear for taste — at whichever point in the cook gives you the best result.
