Cut a hard-boiled egg and sometimes the yolk is a clean, sunny yellow. Other times it wears a dull grey-green halo, and the kitchen smells faintly of sulfur. The ring isn't mould, and it isn't a sign the egg was bad. It is a small, predictable piece of chemistry — and once you understand it, it is easy to avoid.

## What the ring is

The discolouration is **iron sulfide** (specifically ferrous sulfide), a dark compound that looks greenish-grey against the yellow of the yolk.

Two ingredients are needed to make it, and the egg supplies both:

- **Sulfur**, which comes mostly from the egg white. Several white proteins contain sulfur-bearing amino acids, and when they are heated strongly, some of that sulfur is released as **hydrogen sulfide** — the gas behind the "eggy" smell of overcooked eggs.
- **Iron**, which is concentrated in the yolk.

As the white heats, hydrogen sulfide forms and moves through the egg. The yolk in the centre is the coolest part, so the gas migrates inward. Where it meets the iron at the surface of the yolk, the two react and deposit iron sulfide. That is why the ring forms as a thin band exactly at the boundary between white and yolk, rather than throughout the egg.

## What makes it worse

**Overcooking.** The longer the egg spends at high temperature, the more hydrogen sulfide the white releases, and the more iron sulfide forms. A large egg that is perfectly hard-cooked after 11–12 minutes in simmering water will have a much more obvious ring after 20.

**High heat.** A furious boil pushes the outer white far hotter, for longer, than it needs to be. Gentle simmering or steaming produces less sulfur.

**Slow cooling.** An egg left to cool on the bench, or in its hot cooking water, keeps cooking from the inside. The reaction carries on while the egg stays hot.

**Older eggs.** As eggs age, carbon dioxide escapes through the shell and the white becomes more alkaline, rising from a pH of roughly 7.6 in a freshly laid egg to above 9. The release of hydrogen sulfide from egg-white proteins is faster in alkaline conditions, so older eggs discolour more readily. (The same rise in pH is what makes older eggs easier to peel — more on that in [why fresh eggs are hard to peel](/why-fresh-eggs-are-hard-to-peel/).)

## Is it safe to eat?

Yes. Iron sulfide in this amount is harmless. A green-ringed yolk is a sign of overcooking, not spoilage — the main downsides are appearance, a slightly sulfurous flavour, and a drier, chalkier yolk texture, which comes from the same overcooking.

Do still follow normal egg safety: refrigerate hard-boiled eggs within a couple of hours of cooking and eat them within about a week.

## How to avoid the green ring

You don't need special tricks — just control heat and time, then stop the cooking decisively.

1. **Start in hot water or steam.** Bring a pot of water to the boil first, then lower the eggs in gently with a spoon and reduce to a steady simmer. Starting hot makes your timing repeatable and, as a bonus, helps the shell come away more cleanly. A steamer basket over boiling water works just as well and is gentler.
2. **Time it.** For a standard large egg (around 55–60 g) straight from the fridge, about 10–11 minutes at a simmer gives a fully set yolk that is still bright and slightly moist; 12–13 minutes gives a firmer, paler yolk. Smaller eggs need less time.
3. **Shock in iced water.** Move the eggs directly into a bowl of water and ice and leave them for at least 5 minutes. This stops the cooking immediately and pulls heat out of the yolk before the reaction can continue.
4. **Peel and use, or refrigerate.** Once cold, the eggs are ready.

:::tip Batch cooking
If you're cooking a dozen or more eggs at once, use a bigger pot of water so the temperature doesn't crash when they go in, and add a minute or so to the time. Check one egg before you cool the whole batch.
:::

## Can you fix a ring once it's there?

No — the iron sulfide is already formed. But green-ringed eggs are perfectly usable in dishes where the colour doesn't matter: chopped into egg salad, mashed into a sandwich filling, or grated over asparagus. If the yolk is very dry, a little mayonnaise or olive oil brings back some richness.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Kitchens that go through dozens of boiled eggs a day — for salads, garnishes or a breakfast service — usually standardise everything: the same egg grade, the same starting temperature, a measured volume of water, a fixed time and an ice bath ready before the eggs go in. It is less about secret technique than removing variables, so every egg comes out the same.
:::

## Common questions

### Does adding salt or vinegar to the water prevent the ring?

No. Salt and vinegar can help a cracked egg's white set quickly so less leaks out, but the green ring depends on how long and how hot the egg is cooked and how fast it cools. Timing and an ice bath are what prevent it.

### Why do overcooked eggs smell so strong?

The smell is hydrogen sulfide, the same gas that helps form the ring. The longer and hotter eggs cook, the more of it the whites release. Gently cooked eggs that are cooled quickly smell much milder.

### Can other egg dishes turn green?

Yes. Scrambled eggs kept hot for a long time — on a buffet or in a bain-marie — can develop a greenish tint for the same reason: prolonged heat releases sulfur that reacts with iron in the yolk. Caterers avoid it by cooking scrambled eggs in small batches and holding them for as short a time as possible.

## The takeaway

The green ring is simple chemistry: sulfur from an overheated white reacting with iron in the yolk. Keep the heat gentle, cook only as long as you need, and cool the eggs quickly in iced water, and your yolks will stay yellow.
