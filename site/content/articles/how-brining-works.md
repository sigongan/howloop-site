Brined chicken and pork are noticeably juicier than unbrined ones — not because of a trick, but because salt changes the proteins that hold water in meat. Understanding what salt is doing makes it easy to choose between a wet brine and a dry one, and to avoid the mistakes that leave meat spongy or too salty.

## How meat holds water

Muscle is made of long fibres packed with protein filaments, mainly **actin and myosin**, arranged in a tight, regular lattice. Most of the water in meat is held in the spaces between those filaments. The more open the lattice, the more water it can hold; when the filaments pack together more tightly, water is squeezed out.

Cooking makes those proteins contract, which is why meat shrinks and loses juice as it heats (see [does searing seal in juices](/does-searing-seal-in-juices/)). Anything that helps the lattice hold more water before and during cooking means more juice left at the end.

## What salt does

When salt dissolves in meat, its sodium and chloride ions interact with the protein filaments. Two things happen:

1. **The filaments swell.** Chloride ions in particular bind to the proteins and increase the repulsion between neighbouring filaments, pushing them further apart. The lattice opens up and can hold more water. Classic research by Offer and Trinick showed myofibrils — the bundles of filaments inside each muscle fibre — swelling markedly in salt solutions of the strength used in meat processing.
2. **Some proteins partly dissolve.** Salt makes part of the myosin soluble. Dissolved proteins form a gel when they're heated, trapping water rather than letting it run out.

The combined effect is meat that starts with more water — wet-brined meat can gain several per cent of its weight — and loses less during cooking. Salt also changes how muscle proteins react to heat, so they contract less forcefully.

There's a bonus: the meat is **seasoned all the way through**, not just on the surface.

## Wet brining

A wet brine is a solution of salt (and often sugar and aromatics) in water. The meat is submerged and refrigerated for anything from 30 minutes for fish or small cuts to 12–24 hours for a whole bird.

Typical home brines are around **3–6% salt by weight** — for example 50 g of salt in 1 litre of water is 5%. The stronger the brine, the shorter the time needed.

**Strengths:** maximum juiciness, especially for lean meat like chicken breast, turkey and pork loin.

**Weaknesses:** the extra water can make flavour taste slightly diluted and texture slightly soft or "ham-like" if overdone; poultry skin absorbs water and is harder to crisp; and you need space in the fridge for a big container.

:::tip The equilibrium brine
Professional and home cooks increasingly use an **equilibrium brine**: weigh the meat and the water together, then add salt equal to about 1–1.5% of that total weight. Because the meat can only ever reach the concentration of the whole system, it can't become too salty no matter how long it sits — you simply wait longer for the salt to spread through. It is a forgiving method for large cuts.
:::

## Dry brining

A dry brine is simply **salting meat well ahead of cooking** — usually about 1% of the meat's weight in salt (10 g per kilo), spread evenly over the surface, then refrigerated uncovered for anything from an hour to two days.

At first, salt draws moisture out of the surface. Give it time, though, and the salt dissolves in that liquid, which is then drawn back into the meat as the proteins loosen. After roughly 40 minutes to an hour the surface moisture starts to disappear again, and after a day or so the salt has spread well into the meat.

**Strengths:** deep seasoning without added water, so the meat tastes more of itself; a dry surface that browns quickly and gives crisp poultry skin; and no tub of brine in the fridge.

**Weaknesses:** slightly less juiciness gain than a wet brine for very lean cuts, and it needs planning.

Avoid cooking during the awkward middle stage — roughly 10 to 40 minutes after salting — when the surface is wet with drawn-out moisture. Either salt immediately before cooking or give it at least 40 minutes.

## How much salt — and which salt?

Always **measure salt by weight**, not volume. A cup of fine table salt weighs far more than a cup of flaky sea salt or coarse kosher salt, so a recipe written in cups or spoons for one salt can be badly wrong with another.

Sugar in a brine is optional. It adds a touch of sweetness and helps browning, but too much makes skin burn before meat is cooked.

## What brining won't fix

- **Overcooking.** Brining gives you a buffer, not immunity. A brined chicken breast cooked far past done will still be dry.
- **Tough cuts.** Brining helps lean, tender cuts stay moist. It doesn't break down connective tissue — that takes long, slow cooking (see [why braised meat gets tender](/why-braised-meat-gets-tender/)).
- **Already-salted meat.** Many supermarket turkeys and some pork products are injected with a salt solution ("enhanced" or "self-basting"). Brining those again makes them too salty. Check the label.

## Food safety

Brining is done cold. Keep meat and brine in the fridge at 5°C or below, use a food-safe container, discard used brine, and don't reuse it for anything else. Rinsing brined meat under the tap isn't necessary and can splash bacteria around the sink; patting it dry with paper towel is enough.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Kitchens often dry-brine proteins as part of the morning prep: chicken thighs, pork chops and whole birds are weighed, salted by percentage and laid on racks in the coolroom so they are both seasoned and dry by service. Large pork and poultry cuts may go into an equilibrium brine the day before. Working by weight and percentage means the result is the same no matter who does the prep.
:::

## The takeaway

Salt opens up and partly dissolves muscle proteins, so meat holds more water when it is cooked. Use a wet brine when you want maximum juiciness in lean meat, a dry brine when you want deep seasoning and crisp skin, measure salt by weight, and keep everything cold.
