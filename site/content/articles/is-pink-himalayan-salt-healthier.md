Pink salt has moved from health-food shops to every supermarket shelf, usually with claims about "84 trace minerals", detox, hydration or balance. It's pretty, it has a lovely crunch as a finishing salt — but is it actually better for you? An Australian study gives a clear answer.

## What pink salt is

Most "Himalayan" pink salt is mined from ancient salt deposits in the Salt Range of Pakistan's Punjab province. Its pink to orange colour comes from traces of other minerals, particularly iron compounds, mixed in with the salt crystals. Pink salts are also sold from other places, including Peru.

Like any salt, it is overwhelmingly **sodium chloride**. The minerals that give it its colour make up only a small fraction of its weight.

## What the Australian study found

In 2020, researchers led by Flavia Fayet-Moore of Nutrition Research Australia published the first detailed analysis of pink salts sold in Australia, in the journal *Foods*. They bought **31 pink salt products** from supermarkets, greengrocers and health-food shops in Sydney, Canberra and a regional town, plus an iodised white table salt for comparison, and measured 25 minerals in each.

**Sodium.** Pink salt contained on average about 39.5% sodium by weight, compared with about 42.8% in the white salt. A teaspoon (about 5 g) of pink salt still delivers nearly 2,000 mg of sodium — roughly the whole daily limit the World Health Organization recommends.

**Other minerals.** Pink salt did contain significantly more calcium, iron, magnesium, manganese and potassium than white salt. But the amounts are tiny in nutritional terms. Using the study's figures, a full 5 g teaspoon of pink salt provides roughly:

| Mineral | Amount in 5 g of pink salt | Share of a typical adult's daily needs |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium | about 13 mg | about 1% |
| Iron | about 0.3 mg | about 2–4% |
| Magnesium | about 13 mg | about 3–4% |
| Potassium | about 12 mg | well under 1% |

To get meaningful amounts of these minerals from pink salt, you'd have to eat far more salt than is safe. A glass of milk, a handful of nuts or a banana supplies much more.

**Contaminants.** The study also tested for minerals that are undesirable in food. One sample — a fine-grain pink salt from Peru — contained **lead above the maximum level set by Food Standards Australia New Zealand** (about 2.6 mg/kg against a limit of 2 mg/kg). Mineral content also varied widely from product to product and couldn't be predicted from the salt's colour.

The authors concluded that eating enough pink salt to get a meaningful amount of these nutrients would mean eating a harmful amount of sodium, and that pink salt should be eaten within the usual salt guidelines like any other salt.

## The iodine question

There is one area where pink salt is clearly worse: **iodine**. Iodine is essential for thyroid function and brain development, and iodised table salt is one of the main ways many countries prevent deficiency. Most pink salt is not iodised.

In Australia and New Zealand, the salt used to make most bread is required to be iodised (organic bread is exempt), which provides a baseline intake for people who eat bread. But if you switch all your household salt to pink salt — especially if you're pregnant, breastfeeding or don't eat much bread — you may lose an important source of iodine. If you like pink salt, consider keeping iodised salt for everyday cooking.

## Does it taste different?

Dissolved in a soup or pasta water, pink salt tastes essentially the same as any other salt, because it's nearly all sodium chloride. Where it does differ is **texture**: coarse pink salt crystals or flakes give a crunchy burst of salinity when sprinkled on a finished dish. That's a real culinary reason to use it — the same reason chefs use flaky sea salt.

## So which salt should you use?

- **For cooking, baking and seasoning water:** any fine salt works. Iodised salt is a sensible default for most households.
- **For finishing:** flaky sea salt or coarse pink salt adds crunch and visual contrast to steak, salads, chocolate desserts or roasted vegetables.
- **For health:** the type of salt matters far less than the amount. Most adults eat more salt than recommended, much of it from bread, processed meats, sauces and snacks.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Kitchens usually keep two or three salts: an inexpensive fine or kosher-style salt for seasoning as they cook, a flaky salt for finishing, and sometimes a specialty salt for a particular dish. Choices are made on texture, how easily the salt can be pinched and measured, and price — not on mineral claims. Recipes are written by weight, because different salts have very different volumes per gram.
:::

## Common questions

### Is sea salt healthier than table salt?

The same logic applies. Sea salts are also mostly sodium chloride, with trace minerals in nutritionally tiny amounts, and most aren't iodised. Flaky sea salt can contain less sodium *per teaspoon* simply because its large crystals pack loosely, so a spoonful weighs less — gram for gram, the sodium is about the same.

### Does pink salt "detox" the body or balance electrolytes?

There is no good evidence that pink salt, or any salt, removes toxins; that is the job of your liver and kidneys. Salt does supply sodium and chloride, which are electrolytes, but most diets already contain more sodium than recommended.

### Should I worry about lead in pink salt?

The Australian study found one product above the legal limit, and mineral content varied widely between brands. Eaten in normal seasoning amounts, the exposure from salt is small, but there's no way to tell products apart by looking at them — another reason not to eat pink salt in large amounts as a "supplement".

## The takeaway

Pink salt is still salt. Its extra minerals are real but nutritionally trivial at sensible intakes, it delivers nearly as much sodium as table salt, it usually lacks iodine, and at least one product tested in Australia exceeded the lead limit. Enjoy it for its crunch and colour, but don't pay extra for health benefits it can't provide.
