Browning is where much of the flavour in cooking comes from: the crust on bread, the sear on a steak, roasted coffee, toasted nuts, golden onions, caramel. It's common to call all of it "caramelising", but there are really two different families of reactions at work. Knowing which is which explains a lot of kitchen technique.

## The Maillard reaction

The **Maillard reaction** is named after the French chemist Louis-Camille Maillard, who described it in 1912. It is a reaction between:

- a **reducing sugar** — such as glucose, fructose or lactose (milk sugar); and
- an **amino group**, most often from a free amino acid or the end of a protein chain.

The first step joins the sugar and amino acid together. What follows is a sprawling network of further reactions — rearrangements, breakdowns and recombinations — producing hundreds of different compounds. Some are small, volatile aroma molecules with roasted, nutty, meaty, malty or toasty smells. Others link up into large brown pigments called **melanoidins**.

Because different amino acids and sugars give different products, Maillard browning tastes different in different foods: the crust of a roast is not the same as the crust of bread or the aroma of roasted coffee, though all three are mainly Maillard.

## Caramelisation

**Caramelisation** needs only sugar. When sugar is heated hard enough, its molecules break apart and recombine, producing new flavour compounds — buttery, nutty, fruity and eventually bitter — and brown polymers. No protein is involved.

Different sugars caramelise at different temperatures. Fructose starts to break down at around 110°C, glucose at about 150–160°C and ordinary table sugar (sucrose) at roughly 160–170°C. Sucrose first splits into glucose and fructose, which then go on to react. Both acidity and alkalinity speed caramelisation up.

This is the chemistry of caramel sauce, toffee, the burnt-sugar top of a crème brûlée and the dark edges of a pavlova.

## The key difference — and the key similarity

| | Maillard reaction | Caramelisation |
|---|---|---|
| What reacts | Reducing sugars + amino acids/proteins | Sugars alone |
| Typical foods | Seared meat, bread crust, roasted coffee, toasted nuts, chips | Caramel, toffee, brûlée top, spun sugar |
| Flavours | Roasted, savoury, meaty, malty, toasty | Sweet, buttery, nutty, then bitter |
| Practical speed | Fast above about 140°C; slow at lower temperatures | Needs high temperatures for most sugars |

The similarity is **water**. Both reactions are far faster at the high temperatures that only a dry surface can reach. As long as a food is wet, its surface can't get much hotter than water's boiling point, 100°C — the energy goes into evaporating water instead. That is why boiled meat goes grey rather than brown, and why every browning technique starts with drying.

## Maillard happens at lower temperatures too — just slowly

The reaction doesn't have a sharp starting temperature. Below 100°C it still happens, only much more slowly. Given weeks, it can brown foods at modest temperatures — black garlic is made by holding whole garlic warm and humid for weeks, and slow Maillard reactions contribute to the colour and flavour of aged foods. In normal cooking, though, you need high surface temperatures for browning to happen in minutes.

## Caramelised onions are mostly Maillard

Here is a fun twist: "caramelised" onions are not mainly caramelised. Onions contain plenty of sugars, but also amino acids, so slow-cooked onions brown largely through the Maillard reaction, with some true caramelisation as well. The same is true of many "caramelised" vegetables.

That also explains a cook's trick: **a pinch of bicarbonate of soda** speeds up onion browning. The Maillard reaction runs faster in alkaline conditions, so a little baking soda gets onions deeply brown in less time — although it also softens them, so use only a tiny amount.

## How to get better browning

1. **Dry the surface.** Pat meat and vegetables dry; salt meat ahead and leave it uncovered in the fridge so its surface dries.
2. **Use enough heat.** A properly preheated heavy pan, oven or grill gets the surface well above 100°C quickly.
3. **Don't crowd the pan.** Too much food releases too much steam and drops the temperature, so it stews instead of browns.
4. **Add sugar or protein to help.** A brush of egg wash on pastry adds proteins and sugars that brown beautifully. Milk solids in butter brown to make beurre noisette (brown butter). A little honey or sugar in a glaze speeds browning.
5. **Use a touch of alkali where suitable.** Pretzels get their deep brown crust from a dip in an alkaline bath before baking.

## A note on acrylamide and burning

Browning is about flavour, but the same chemistry can go too far. When certain sugars react with the amino acid asparagine at high temperatures, one product is acrylamide, a compound that agencies such as the UK Food Standards Agency advise limiting by cooking starchy foods to a golden rather than dark brown colour. Very dark or burnt food also tastes bitter. Aim for deep golden-brown, not black.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Chefs use both reactions deliberately. A sauce might begin with well-browned bones and vegetables for Maillard depth, while a dessert section might take sugar to a precise caramel temperature and colour, then stop it instantly with cream or butter. Brown butter is a staple too — milk solids toasted in butterfat give a nutty Maillard flavour to sauces, cakes and pasta.
:::

## The takeaway

The Maillard reaction is sugar plus protein, responsible for most savoury browning; caramelisation is sugar on its own, responsible for caramel. Both need a dry surface well above 100°C — so dry your food, use proper heat, give it space, and stop at deep golden.
