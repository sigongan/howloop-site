"Salt the water like the sea" is one of the most repeated lines in cooking. So is the idea that salt makes the water boil hotter, and that a splash of oil stops pasta sticking. Only one of those three ideas really holds up — and a fourth, less famous fact about pasta water matters more than all of them.

## Does salt make water boil hotter?

Technically yes, practically no. Dissolving salt in water raises its boiling point, but by a tiny amount. We can work it out:

- The boiling point rises by about 0.51°C for each mole of dissolved particles per kilogram of water.
- Salt (sodium chloride) splits into two particles, sodium and chloride.
- 10 g of salt is about 0.17 moles, which gives about 0.34 moles of particles in a litre of water.
- 0.34 × 0.51 ≈ **0.17°C**.

So a well-salted pot of pasta water boils less than two-tenths of a degree hotter than plain water. Even at seawater strength, the difference is only about 0.6°C. That won't change how the pasta cooks.

What you may notice is that the water foams up or boils vigorously for a moment when you add salt. That's because the salt crystals give dissolved gases and steam somewhere to form bubbles, not because the water got hotter.

## Why salt matters: seasoning from the inside

Dried pasta absorbs a lot of water as it cooks — 100 g of dry pasta typically comes out weighing around 220–250 g. That water is drawn deep into the pasta, along with whatever is dissolved in it. Salted water seasons the pasta *throughout*, which gives it a fuller flavour that no amount of salt in the sauce can replicate. Unsalted pasta tastes flat, even under a well-seasoned sauce.

## How much salt?

Many chefs and Italian cooks use about **10 g of salt per litre of water** — roughly 1%. Some go up to 12–15 g for plain sauces where the pasta itself carries a lot of the flavour. A good test: taste the water. It should taste pleasantly seasoned, like a well-seasoned broth — not like the ocean.

"Salty as the sea" is a poor guide. Seawater is around 3.5% salt, which would make most pasta unpleasantly salty.

Adjust for the sauce: if it's built on salty ingredients like pecorino, anchovies, capers, olives or cured pork, use a little less salt in the water.

How much of that salt do you actually eat? Not all of it. As a rough upper limit, if 100 g of dry pasta takes up about 120–150 g of 1% salted water, that's at most about 1.2–1.5 g of salt per serve — and in practice less, because much of the salt is drained away with the water and some of the absorbed water evaporates.

## When to add the salt

Add salt once the water is boiling. It dissolves almost instantly in hot water, and it means you aren't leaving undissolved salt sitting on the bottom of a stainless-steel pot — many cookware makers recommend this, because salt resting on the metal before it dissolves can cause small pits in the surface over time. Then add the pasta.

## Oil in the water: skip it

Oil floats on top of the water, so it barely touches the pasta while it cooks. It doesn't stop sticking. Worse, a little oil can coat the pasta as you drain it, so sauce clings less well.

What actually stops sticking:

- **Stir during the first minute or two.** That's when the outside of the pasta is releasing starch and is most likely to glue together.
- **Use enough water to move freely.** You don't need a giant pot, but the pasta should be able to circulate.
- **Don't rinse cooked pasta** (unless it's for a cold salad). Rinsing washes off the surface starch that helps sauce stick.

## The real secret: starchy pasta water

As pasta cooks, it releases starch into the water. That cloudy, slightly salty water is one of the most useful ingredients in Italian cooking.

When you finish pasta in its sauce — tossing it in the pan with a splash of pasta water — the dissolved starch thickens the liquid and acts as a stabiliser, helping fat (olive oil, butter, cheese) and water combine into a glossy, clinging sauce rather than a greasy one. This finishing step is often called *mantecatura*, and it is how dishes like cacio e pepe, aglio e olio and carbonara get their creamy texture without cream. (For more on fat-and-water sauces, see [why sauces split](/why-sauces-split/).)

:::tip How to use pasta water
Before draining, scoop out a mug of the cooking water. Drain the pasta when it's a minute short of done, add it to the sauce with a splash of the water, and toss over the heat until the sauce turns glossy and coats every piece. Add more water a little at a time if it gets too thick.
:::

## Fresh vs dried pasta

Fresh egg pasta absorbs less water and cooks in a few minutes, so it picks up less salt from the water. Salt the water the same way, but expect to season the sauce a little more.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
In busy Italian kitchens, pasta is usually cooked in large pasta boilers that run all night, with salt added by weight at the start of service and topped up as fresh water goes in. Because the same water cooks many portions, it grows starchier through the night — cooks know the sauce comes together more easily as service goes on. Pasta is almost always finished in the pan with its sauce and a ladle of cooking water.
:::

## The takeaway

Salt your pasta water for flavour, not for boiling point — about 10 g per litre is a good starting point. Leave out the oil, stir early, and always save some starchy cooking water to bring your sauce together.
