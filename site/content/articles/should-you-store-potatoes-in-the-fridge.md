For years the standard advice was clear: never put raw potatoes in the fridge. Then, in early 2023, the UK's Food Standards Agency reversed its own guidance and said refrigeration was fine. Both positions rest on the same piece of potato biology, and understanding it helps you store potatoes in a way that suits your kitchen — especially in a warm climate, where a cool, dark cupboard may not exist.

## Potatoes are alive

A potato is a living storage organ. It is packed with starch that the plant would use to sprout in spring, and it keeps breathing slowly while it sits in your pantry. That's why storage conditions matter so much: warmth and light encourage it to sprout and turn green, and cold triggers a different change.

## Cold-induced sweetening

When potatoes are kept below roughly 8–10°C, their metabolism shifts. Enzymes break down some of the starch into sugars, including the "reducing sugars" glucose and fructose. Researchers call this **cold-induced sweetening**. In very cold storage, potatoes can taste noticeably sweet.

For cooking, those extra reducing sugars matter in two ways:

1. **Faster, darker browning.** Reducing sugars are a key ingredient in the Maillard reaction (see [the Maillard reaction vs caramelisation](/maillard-reaction-vs-caramelisation/)). Potatoes high in sugar brown quickly when fried or roasted and can turn dark — even bitter — before they are cooked through. That's why the potato industry stores processing potatoes carefully and tests their sugar levels.
2. **More acrylamide.** When reducing sugars react with the amino acid asparagine at high temperatures, one product is **acrylamide**. Acrylamide forms in many starchy foods cooked above about 120°C — chips, roast potatoes, toast, biscuits and coffee — and international agencies classify it as a probable human carcinogen based on animal studies.

## The old advice

In 2017 the UK Food Standards Agency launched a public campaign called "Go for Gold" to help people reduce acrylamide at home. Alongside advice to cook starchy foods to a golden colour rather than dark brown, it told people not to keep raw potatoes in the fridge if they intended to roast or fry them, and to store them in a cool, dark place above 6°C instead.

## What changed

In 2023 the FSA updated that advice. Its scientific advisers on the Committee on Toxicity reviewed a study comparing potatoes stored in domestic fridges with potatoes stored in a cool, dark place, and concluded that home refrigeration did not substantially increase the potential for acrylamide to form during cooking. The agency now says raw potatoes can be stored either in the fridge or in a cool, dark place — and points out that refrigeration can help reduce food waste, because potatoes keep longer.

The European Food Information Council summarises the current evidence the same way: storing potatoes in a home fridge does not significantly increase their acrylamide-forming potential compared with a cool, dark cupboard.

Why the difference from the theory? Cold-induced sweetening is real, but a likely explanation is that the sugar that builds up over a typical stay in a home fridge is modest — and acrylamide formation depends heavily on **how dark you cook the potato**, whichever way it was stored.

## So where should you keep potatoes?

**Best: cool, dark, dry and ventilated.** Around 7–10°C, away from light, in a paper or hessian bag or a basket. This keeps flavour and texture closest to fresh and slows sprouting.

**Fine: the fridge.** If your kitchen is warm — as it is most of the year in much of Australia — the fridge may keep potatoes in better condition than a warm pantry where they quickly sprout or soften. Some varieties may taste slightly sweeter and brown faster when fried.

**Avoid:**

- **Light**, which turns potatoes green (see below).
- **Sealed plastic bags**, which trap moisture and encourage rot.
- **Freezing raw potatoes**, which ruptures their cells and makes them watery and grainy when thawed. Cooked or blanched potatoes freeze much better.

## Green potatoes and sprouts

Light makes potatoes produce chlorophyll, which turns them green. The green itself is harmless, but it signals that the potato has also been making more **glycoalkaloids** such as solanine — natural toxins that taste bitter and can cause stomach upsets in large amounts. Cut away green patches and sprouts generously, and discard potatoes that are extensively green, shrivelled or taste bitter.

:::tip Cook for colour, not darkness
Whatever your storage method, the most effective way to reduce acrylamide is the FSA's original advice: aim for a golden colour rather than dark brown when frying, roasting or toasting starchy foods. Soaking cut chips in water for a while before cooking also washes away some surface sugar and helps them brown more evenly.
:::

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Restaurants buy potatoes by variety and purpose: waxy types for salads and gratins, floury types for mash and roasting, and specific chipping potatoes for fries. Chippers are kept out of the coolroom where possible, because cooks know that cold-stored potatoes can brown too fast in the fryer. Cooks also rely on blanching — par-cooking chips at a lower temperature, then frying hot to order — to get a golden, evenly cooked result.
:::

## Common questions

### Should potatoes and onions be stored together?

It's commonly advised against. Both prefer cool, dark storage, but potatoes need some humidity while onions prefer dry air, and many cooks find potatoes sprout or spoil sooner when kept next to onions. The evidence for an interaction is limited, but separate storage costs nothing.

### Can I peel and cut potatoes ahead of time?

Yes. Cut raw potatoes brown because of the same enzyme reaction that browns apples (see [why cut apples turn brown](/why-cut-apples-turn-brown/)). Keep them covered in cold water in the fridge and cook them within a day. They lose a little surface starch into the water, which can actually help chips crisp.

### Are sprouted potatoes safe?

Firm potatoes with small sprouts are generally fine once the sprouts, the eyes around them and any green areas are cut away generously. Throw away potatoes that are shrivelled, soft, extensively green or bitter-tasting.

## The takeaway

Cold storage makes potatoes a little sweeter, which once led to advice not to refrigerate them. Newer evidence shows home refrigeration doesn't meaningfully raise acrylamide, so store potatoes wherever keeps them coolest, darkest and driest in your kitchen — and cook them to golden, not dark brown.
