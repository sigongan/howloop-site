Few ingredients have as strange a reputation as MSG. It's in instant noodles, chips and stock cubes, many restaurants advertise "no added MSG", and yet chefs around the world use glutamate-rich ingredients to make food taste deeper and more savoury. So what is it, and what does the evidence actually say?

## What MSG is

**Monosodium glutamate** is the sodium salt of **glutamic acid**, one of the most common amino acids in nature. Glutamic acid is a building block of proteins, and our bodies make it too. When glutamate is bound up in a protein, it has no taste. When it is **free** — released by ripening, ageing, fermenting or cooking — it triggers a savoury taste.

MSG is simply that free glutamate paired with sodium so it forms a stable, easily dissolved crystal. Today it is made by fermentation, using bacteria to convert starches or sugars from sources such as sugar cane, sugar beet or corn into glutamate — much as yoghurt, vinegar or soy sauce are made by fermentation.

The glutamate in MSG is chemically identical to the glutamate in a tomato or a piece of parmesan, and the body handles both in the same way.

## A short history of umami

In 1908 the Japanese chemist **Kikunae Ikeda** set out to identify the savoury flavour of *dashi*, the broth made from kombu seaweed (and often dried bonito). He isolated glutamate from kombu and showed it was responsible for the taste, which he called **umami** — roughly, "delicious savouriness". A year later, MSG went into commercial production in Japan.

For decades, many Western scientists treated umami as a combination of the familiar tastes. Research since then, including the discovery of receptors on the tongue that respond to glutamate, has established umami as a basic taste alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter.

## Where glutamate occurs naturally

Free glutamate is abundant in foods we prize for savoury depth:

- aged cheeses, especially parmesan;
- ripe tomatoes, and even more in tomato paste and sun-dried tomatoes;
- mushrooms, especially dried shiitake;
- kombu and other seaweeds;
- soy sauce, miso, fish sauce and other fermented products;
- anchovies, cured hams and long-cooked meat stocks.

That's why a parmesan rind in minestrone, a spoon of tomato paste in a braise or a dash of fish sauce in a dressing makes such a difference. They are all adding glutamate.

## Umami synergy

Glutamate works even better alongside two other compounds, **inosinate** (found in meat, fish and dried bonito) and **guanylate** (found in dried mushrooms). Together they produce a savoury taste far stronger than either alone. Japanese dashi is the classic example: kombu supplies glutamate and bonito flakes supply inosinate. Many seasoning blends combine MSG with these "ribonucleotides" (food additives 627 and 631) for the same reason.

## Is MSG safe?

Concerns about MSG date back to a 1968 letter to a medical journal describing headache, flushing and other symptoms after Chinese restaurant meals. The idea spread widely and gave rise to the name "Chinese restaurant syndrome", even though no ingredient had been tested.

Since then, regulators have reviewed the evidence repeatedly:

- **Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ)** carried out a safety assessment in 2003 and found no convincing evidence that MSG causes serious, whole-body reactions leading to severe illness or death. It acknowledges that some people may report mild, short-lived symptoms after consuming large amounts.
- **The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)** considers MSG "generally recognised as safe". An independent scientific review it commissioned in the 1990s found that some sensitive individuals may experience short-term, mild symptoms — such as headache or flushing — after consuming 3 g or more of MSG without food. A typical serving of food with added MSG contains well under half a gram.

In controlled, double-blind studies where people don't know whether they are eating MSG, consistent reactions have been hard to reproduce. If you believe you react to MSG, it's reasonable to avoid it; for most people there's no evidence of harm at normal intakes.

## MSG and sodium

Weight for weight, MSG contains about 12% sodium, compared with about 39% in table salt. Because a small amount of MSG makes food taste more savoury and fuller, replacing some of the salt in a recipe with MSG can lower the sodium content without making the dish taste bland.

## How to spot it on a label

In Australia and New Zealand, MSG appears in ingredient lists as **flavour enhancer (621)** or by name. Other glutamates have their own numbers (620–625). Ingredients such as yeast extract, hydrolysed vegetable protein and soy sauce also contain free glutamate naturally and are listed by name. FSANZ notes that "no added MSG" claims are regulated by the Australian Competition and Consumer Commission under consumer law.

## How to use MSG in cooking

- **Use a little.** A pinch — around 0.5 g per serve, or roughly a quarter to half a teaspoon in a pot of soup for four — is plenty. Too much tastes flat and slightly sweet-savoury.
- **Pair it with salt, not instead of salt.** MSG enhances savoury depth; salt is still needed for seasoning.
- **Add it to stocks, soups, braises, dressings, marinades, fried rice and roasted vegetables.** Anywhere you'd reach for a stock cube, parmesan or soy sauce.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Chefs in many cuisines layer umami deliberately, whether or not they use pure MSG: kombu in Japanese stocks, anchovy and parmesan in Italian sauces, fish sauce in Southeast Asian dressings, tomato paste in braises. Some modern kitchens use MSG openly as a seasoning alongside salt; others prefer to build the same effect from glutamate-rich ingredients.
:::

## The takeaway

MSG is a pure form of the glutamate that makes parmesan, tomatoes and soy sauce taste savoury. Regulators in Australia, New Zealand and the US consider it safe at normal intakes, and it can help reduce sodium. Use a small pinch as a tool for flavour — just as cooks have long used glutamate-rich ingredients.
