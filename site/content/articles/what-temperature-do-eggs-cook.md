Ask a room of cooks how long to boil an egg and you'll get a dozen answers. Ask what temperature an egg *cooks* at and most people go quiet. Yet temperature is the real answer to almost every egg question — soft or hard, silky or rubbery, jammy or chalky. Once you know the handful of numbers that matter, every egg technique starts to make sense.

## An egg is two different foods

The white and the yolk look like one ingredient, but chemically they are very different.

The **white** (albumen) is about 88% water and roughly 10–11% protein, with almost no fat. That protein is not one substance but a mixture of dozens, and they react to heat differently. The most important for cooking are:

- **Ovotransferrin**, which makes up around an eighth of the white's protein and starts to set first, at roughly 61–62°C.
- **Ovalbumin**, more than half of the white's protein, which does not set firmly until around 80°C.
- **Lysozyme** and **ovomucoid**, smaller fractions; ovomucoid is notably heat-stable and barely changes during normal cooking.

The **yolk** is about half water, a third fat and a sixth protein, organised into tiny particles held in a watery fluid. Yolk proteins begin to thicken at about 65°C and set into a firm, sliceable mass at around 70°C.

So the white has a long, gradual setting range spread across nearly 20 degrees, while the yolk changes over just a few degrees. That single difference explains most of what happens when you cook an egg.

## What "setting" actually means

Raw proteins are long chains folded into compact shapes, held in place by weak bonds. Heat shakes those bonds apart and the chains unfold — this is called **denaturation**. Unfolded proteins expose sticky parts that were previously tucked inside, so they bump into each other and bond, forming a mesh that traps water. A loose mesh is a tender gel; a tight mesh squeezes water out and becomes rubbery.

That is why gentle heat gives a delicate texture and aggressive heat gives a tough one. It is not that the egg is "more cooked" in some vague sense — the protein network has literally tightened and wrung out its own moisture.

## The temperature ladder

This table shows what you can expect from an egg held at a given temperature long enough to come to equilibrium. These are approximate — eggs vary, and time matters, as explained below.

| Egg temperature | White | Yolk |
|---|---|---|
| 60–62°C | Milky, barely thickened, still loose | Liquid, warm |
| 63–64°C | Soft, trembling gel; parts still runny | Thick and syrupy, like warm honey |
| 65–67°C | Tender and opaque, but delicate | Jammy and spoonable, holds its shape |
| 70°C | Set but soft | Set, moist and fudgy |
| 75–80°C | Firm, fully opaque | Firm and sliceable |
| 85°C and above, for a long time | Rubbery, squeaky | Dry, crumbly, chalky; grey-green edge possible |

Notice something odd at the top: around 63–64°C, the **yolk is more set than the white**. That is the basis of the so-called 63°C egg served in many restaurants, and of Japanese *onsen tamago*, traditionally cooked in hot-spring water. The ovotransferrin has begun to set, but the ovalbumin — most of the white's protein — has not, while the yolk has already started to thicken.

## Why boiled eggs behave differently

Boiling water is about 100°C, far above every number in the table. An egg dropped into it cooks from the outside in, so it is never one temperature throughout. The outer white quickly passes 80°C and firms up, while the centre lags behind. A "six-minute egg" works because you stop the clock while the outer white is set and the yolk in the middle has only reached the mid-60s.

This is also why egg size, starting temperature and pan size change the result so much. A large egg straight from the fridge has further to travel than a small one at room temperature, and a small pot of water drops further in temperature when cold eggs go in. If you want consistent boiled eggs, keep those variables the same every time: same size, same starting temperature, plenty of water, and a timer.

## Time matters too

Protein setting is not an instant switch at one temperature. It depends on both temperature and time. An egg held at 64°C for 45 minutes is softer than the same egg held at 64°C for two hours, because proteins continue to unfold and bond slowly even at a steady temperature. The yolk is especially sensitive: given long enough, it keeps thickening.

That is why precise sous-vide egg recipes always give a time *and* a temperature, and why the same temperature from two different recipes can give slightly different results.

## Practical ways to use these numbers

- **Soft scrambled eggs.** Cook over low heat and pull them off while they still look a little wet. The eggs keep cooking from their own heat, and the curds stay soft because the proteins never get hot enough to tighten fully.
- **Silky omelettes.** A thin layer of egg sets fast. Keep the pan moderate and keep the egg moving until it is barely set, then roll it.
- **Jammy boiled eggs.** Lower eggs into boiling water so you control the start, then cool them in iced water at the exact moment you want, which stops the yolk climbing out of the jammy range.
- **Poached eggs.** Water held at a gentle simmer rather than a rolling boil sets the outside without blasting it into rubber.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Restaurants that serve slow-cooked eggs often cook them in batches in a temperature-controlled water bath, then hold them warm for service. The appeal is consistency: every egg comes out the same, and the tight temperature window is easier to hit with a thermostat than with a saucepan and a timer.
:::

## A note on food safety

Raw and undercooked eggs can carry *Salmonella*. Thorough cooking kills it, and that is why consumer food-safety guidance is more conservative than restaurant practice. The US Department of Agriculture recommends cooking egg dishes to 71°C (160°F) and cooking eggs until both the white and yolk are firm, and it singles out people at higher risk — young children, older people, pregnant people and anyone with a weakened immune system — as needing particular care.

Runny and jammy eggs are a personal choice for healthy adults. If you are cooking for someone in a higher-risk group, cook eggs fully or use pasteurised eggs.

## The takeaway

Eggs are not mysterious once you see them as two sets of proteins with different temperature ranges. The white sets gradually between about 62°C and 80°C; the yolk changes quickly between about 65°C and 70°C. Control the heat, control the time, and you control the texture.
