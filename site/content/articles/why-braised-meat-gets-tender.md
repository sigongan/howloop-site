Beef cheeks, lamb shanks, pork shoulder, osso buco, brisket: the cuts that make the best braises are the toughest pieces of the animal. Grill them like a steak and they are almost inedible. Cook them slowly for hours and they become so tender you can cut them with a spoon. The transformation comes down to one protein — **collagen** — and to time.

## Two kinds of toughness

Meat toughness has two main sources.

**Muscle fibres.** These are the long cells that make up the meat itself. Their proteins tighten as they cook, so muscle fibres get firmer the hotter they get. This is the toughness you notice in an overcooked steak.

**Connective tissue.** Every muscle fibre, every bundle of fibres and the whole muscle is wrapped in sheaths of connective tissue made largely of **collagen**. Muscles that do a lot of work — legs, shoulders, cheeks, the chest — need more of this scaffolding, and their collagen is more heavily cross-linked, which makes it stronger. That is why the hardest-working cuts are the toughest.

Tender cuts like fillet have little connective tissue, so they are best cooked quickly to a low internal temperature. Tough cuts need a completely different approach.

## Why meat gets tougher before it gets tender

If you cook a piece of shin or brisket and taste it at intervals, something counterintuitive happens: it gets **worse** before it gets better.

- Between about 40 and 60°C, the muscle proteins contract and squeeze out water.
- At roughly 55–65°C, collagen **denatures and shrinks**. Its fibres contract sharply, squeezing the muscle bundles they wrap and pushing out a great deal of juice. The exact temperature depends on the animal and on how fast the meat is heated; a review by meat scientist Peter Purslow notes it is usually 62–67°C when heated quickly, and can occur at 55–60°C with slow heating.

At this stage, a tough cut is at its worst: it has lost much of its moisture and the shrunken collagen makes it chewy.

## The magic: collagen to gelatin

Keep cooking, and the shrunken collagen begins to break down. Its tightly wound strands unravel and dissolve into **gelatin**, a soft, water-loving protein. This conversion is slow: it speeds up as temperature rises above about 70°C and generally takes hours rather than minutes in a typical braise.

As the collagen dissolves, two good things happen:

1. **The muscle fibres fall apart.** With their connective sheaths dissolved, the fibres separate easily under a fork — the classic "falling off the bone" texture.
2. **The meat feels juicy again.** The muscle fibres themselves are thoroughly cooked and have lost water, but gelatin coats them and holds liquid, giving a moist, silky, rich mouthfeel. The braising liquid also becomes glossy and body-rich from the dissolved gelatin.

That is why a properly braised shank tastes succulent even though its internal temperature has been well above "well done" for hours.

## How to braise well

**Keep it gentle.** A braise should barely simmer — a few lazy bubbles — not boil hard. Most of the meat sits in the liquid at around 85–95°C. A vigorous boil heats the outside of the meat more aggressively, tightening and drying the muscle fibres further, and it can make the meat stringy before the collagen has dissolved.

**Use a low oven.** An oven at around 140–160°C with the pot covered gives steady, even heat from all sides and needs less attention than the stovetop.

**Give it time.** Two to four hours is typical for cheeks, shanks and shoulder, depending on size. Test by texture, not the clock: a skewer should slide in with almost no resistance.

**Brown first for flavour.** Searing the meat and vegetables before adding liquid builds flavour through the Maillard reaction. It doesn't seal anything in, but it makes the sauce deeper.

**Let it cool in the liquid.** Braised meat is often better the next day. Cooling it in its braising liquid lets the meat absorb some of that gelatin-rich liquid, and it makes the fat easy to lift off once chilled.

:::tip Pressure cookers and slow cookers
A pressure cooker raises the boiling point to around 115–120°C, which speeds collagen breakdown dramatically, so shanks or cheeks can be tender in under an hour — at the cost of some control over texture. A slow cooker does the opposite: lower, gentler heat over a longer time, which is forgiving but can leave sauces thin because little evaporates.
:::

## The low-temperature alternative

Collagen doesn't need high heat to break down — it needs time. Held for a long time at a lower temperature, it slowly dissolves too. That is the idea behind long sous-vide cooks such as beef short ribs held at around 60°C for a day or more: the collagen softens while the muscle never gets hot enough to lose as much water, giving meat that is tender yet still pink. Long, low cooks like this rely on precise equipment and careful food-safety practice.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Braises are a prep-kitchen staple because they reward cooking ahead. Cheeks, shanks and ragù bases are usually cooked in large batches a day before, cooled in their liquid, portioned cold (when the meat holds its shape and the fat has set on top), then reheated in the sauce to order. In Italian kitchens, osso buco and slow-cooked ragù are classic examples — both rely on hours of gentle heat, not on any shortcut.
:::

## The takeaway

Tough cuts are tough because of collagen. Heat first makes them tougher as collagen shrinks and squeezes out juice; only time at gentle heat turns that collagen into gelatin, which loosens the fibres and makes the meat feel moist again. Braise low, braise long, and judge doneness by tenderness, not by the clock.
