Leave a loaf on the bench for a couple of days and the crumb turns firm, crumbly and dry-tasting. The obvious explanation is that it has dried out. That is part of the story, but not the main part — and the real cause explains two kitchen puzzles: why bread goes stale *faster* in the fridge, and why a stale loaf can taste fresh again after a few minutes in the oven.

## What baking does to starch

Flour is mostly starch, stored in tiny, tightly packed granules. In those granules the two kinds of starch molecule — **amylose** (long, straight chains) and **amylopectin** (large, highly branched molecules) — are partly organised into crystal-like regions.

When dough is baked, the granules soak up water from the dough and swell, and the heat breaks down their ordered structure. This is **gelatinisation**. The starch becomes soft, disordered and full of water, and together with the gluten network it gives fresh bread its soft, springy crumb.

## What staling is

As bread cools and sits, the starch molecules slowly begin to line up again and re-form ordered, crystalline regions. This is called **retrogradation**. As the starch re-crystallises, it stiffens and holds water differently, so the crumb becomes firm and crumbly and feels dry in the mouth — even if the loaf has lost very little water overall.

It happens in two stages:

- **Amylose** retrogrades quickly, mostly within hours of baking as the bread cools. This sets the crumb and is why freshly baked bread is easier to slice once it has cooled.
- **Amylopectin** retrogrades slowly, over days. This is the main cause of staling during storage.

At the same time, water migrates within the loaf — from the starch towards the gluten, and from the moist crumb towards the drier crust — which is why a crisp crust turns leathery as bread ages.

A 2014 review of the research by Fadda and colleagues confirmed this picture: amylopectin retrogradation and the redistribution of water are central to staling, with proteins and other flour components also playing a part.

## Why the fridge makes it worse

Retrogradation has an awkward temperature profile. It is fastest at cool temperatures just above freezing — the range of a typical refrigerator — and slower at room temperature. Below freezing, the water in the bread becomes ice, molecules can barely move and staling almost stops.

So bread stored in the fridge stales much faster than bread on the bench, while bread in the freezer barely stales at all.

## The catch: mould

The fridge does slow mould. In a hot, humid climate — much of tropical and subtropical Australia, for example — bread on the bench can go mouldy in a couple of days. That leaves a trade-off: the bench for texture, the fridge for mould. The freezer solves both problems.

## How to store bread

- **Eating it within a day or two:** keep it at room temperature, cut side down on a board or in a bread bag or box. Crusty bread keeps its crust better in paper or cloth; soft sandwich bread stays softer in plastic.
- **Keeping it longer:** slice it and freeze it in a sealed bag. Toast slices straight from frozen, or thaw at room temperature.
- **Avoid the fridge** unless mould is a real problem; if it is, the freezer is still the better choice.

## How to revive stale bread

Retrogradation is reversible. Heating bread above about 60°C melts the starch crystals again and re-softens the crumb — the same gelatinisation that happened during baking, on a smaller scale.

1. Lightly wet the crust by running it under the tap for a second or brushing it with water.
2. Heat it in an oven at 150–180°C for 5–10 minutes, depending on size, until the crust is crisp and the inside is warm.
3. Eat it soon. Revived bread stales again quickly as it cools, often faster than the first time.

Toasting works on the same principle, which is why stale bread still makes excellent toast.

:::tip Make the most of stale bread
Many great dishes were invented for bread past its best: Italian panzanella and ribollita, pangrattato (toasted breadcrumbs), bread-and-butter pudding, French toast, croutons, stuffing and Spanish migas. Firm, slightly stale bread often works better than fresh in these, because it holds its shape when soaked.
:::

## Why some bread stays soft longer

Commercial sandwich bread often stays soft for days because manufacturers add ingredients such as enzymes, emulsifiers or fats that slow retrogradation or retain moisture. Breads enriched with butter, milk, eggs or sugar also tend to stale more slowly than lean breads made from just flour, water, salt and yeast. Sourdough's acidity and long fermentation can slow staling too.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Restaurants that serve bread usually bake or buy it daily and refresh it in a hot oven just before it goes to the table. Leftover bread rarely goes to waste: it becomes croutons, crumbs for crumbing and gratins, thickener for soups such as pappa al pomodoro, or the base of a panzanella. And bread for service is kept at room temperature or frozen — almost never refrigerated.
:::

## Common questions

### Why do crackers and biscuits go soft while bread goes hard?

They're moving in opposite directions. Crackers and biscuits are drier than the air around them, so they absorb moisture and lose their crispness. Bread is moister than the air, and its starch re-crystallises as it ages, so it firms up. Both are best kept in airtight containers — for different reasons.

### Is stale bread safe to eat?

Yes. Staling changes texture, not safety. Mould is different: discard bread with visible mould, because mould threads can spread through soft bread further than you can see.

### Does toasting bread make it less stale?

Toasting heats the bread enough to reverse retrogradation at the surface and crisps it, so stale bread toasts well. It's one of the simplest ways to use bread that's a few days old.

## The takeaway

Bread goes stale mainly because its starch slowly re-crystallises after baking, and that happens fastest at fridge temperatures. Keep bread at room temperature for a day or two, freeze it for longer, and revive stale bread with a quick splash of water and a hot oven.
