A good custard is one of the great tricks of cooking: a few eggs turn a jug of milk into something thick, smooth and glossy. A few degrees too far, and the same mixture becomes sweet scrambled eggs floating in thin liquid. The difference between the two is mostly temperature — and knowing where the line is.

## What makes a custard thicken

Custards are thickened by **egg proteins**. As they heat, those proteins unfold and link up into a loose network that traps the surrounding liquid, turning it from runny to creamy. (For how this works in a plain egg, see [what temperature eggs cook at](/what-temperature-do-eggs-cook/).)

In a custard, though, the egg is heavily diluted. A crème anglaise might use six yolks to half a litre or more of milk and cream. The proteins are spread far apart, so they need more heat and more time to find each other and bond. Sugar dilutes them further and also interferes with protein bonding, raising the setting temperature again.

The result is that while egg yolk alone sets at around 70°C, a typical stirred custard only begins to thicken properly in the high 70s and is usually finished at about 80–84°C. That gives you a wonderful, creamy texture — but it also puts you only a few degrees away from trouble.

## What curdling is

If the custard keeps heating, the protein network does not just hold the liquid — it tightens. Proteins bond more and more strongly to one another, pulling into small dense clumps and squeezing out the water they were holding. You see this as grainy texture, visible lumps and a thin, watery liquid around them. Cooks call it curdling or splitting; food scientists call the squeezing-out of liquid **syneresis**.

Curdling usually starts somewhere above about 85°C in a stirred custard. The danger is greatest at the bottom and sides of the pan, where the metal is far hotter than the average temperature of the mixture. That is why a custard can look fine on top while lumps are forming underneath.

## Why starch changes everything

Pastry cream (crème pâtissière) contains eggs too, yet recipes tell you to bring it to the boil. It doesn't curdle because it also contains **flour or cornflour**.

Starch granules swell as they absorb water and burst into long molecules that thicken the liquid and physically get in the way of the egg proteins, stopping them from bonding into tight clumps. With enough starch, the custard can be boiled safely.

In fact, pastry cream *needs* to boil. Egg yolks contain an enzyme, amylase, that breaks down starch. If the cream isn't heated enough to destroy it, the enzyme can quietly digest the starch and turn a thick cream runny after it has cooled. Bringing it to a boil for a minute or two, while whisking, solves that.

## How to make a silky stirred custard

1. **Temper the eggs.** Heat the milk and cream first, then whisk a little of the hot liquid into the yolks and sugar before combining everything. This warms the yolks gradually rather than shocking them.
2. **Use low to moderate heat and a heavy pan.** A thick-based pan spreads heat evenly and reduces hot spots.
3. **Stir constantly, scraping the bottom and corners.** A flat-ended spatula reaches the edges better than a whisk or round spoon.
4. **Use a thermometer.** Stop at 80–84°C. If you don't have one, use the classic "nappe" test: the custard should coat the back of a spoon, and a line drawn through it with a finger should stay clear.
5. **Have a cold bowl ready.** Pour the finished custard straight through a fine sieve into a bowl set over ice. The pan holds a lot of heat, and leaving the custard in it can push it over the edge after you've turned the heat off.

## Baked custards

Crème caramel, crème brûlée and baked cheesecakes face the same problem in the oven. The usual solution is a **water bath** (bain-marie): the dish sits in hot water, which can't rise above 100°C and protects the edges from the oven's much hotter air. Take baked custards out when the edges are set but the centre still wobbles slightly — it will finish setting as it cools.

Overbaked custards show the same symptoms as overcooked stirred ones: bubbles, a weeping layer of liquid and a grainy texture.

## How to rescue a curdled custard

If you catch it early — just a slight graininess — take it off the heat immediately, pour it into a cold bowl and whisk hard, or use a stick blender. Blending breaks the clumps into particles small enough that the texture becomes smooth again, and straining removes what's left. The custard may be a little thinner, but it is often perfectly usable.

If it has fully split into firm curds and watery liquid, it cannot really be saved as a sauce. Use it as a base for bread pudding or blend it into an ice cream mix, where the texture matters less.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Pastry sections often cook crème anglaise to a specific temperature with a probe thermometer rather than by eye, and pour it into a container sitting in iced water straight away. Some kitchens cook it in a temperature-controlled water bath or a thermal mixer for the same reason — a custard that reaches 82°C and stops there is the same every time.
:::

## A note on food safety

Bringing a custard to 80°C or more also takes care of safety: the US Department of Agriculture recommends that egg dishes reach at least 71°C. Cool custards quickly and keep them refrigerated, and use pasteurised eggs for any uncooked egg preparations.

## The takeaway

Custard is a balancing act between enough heat to set diluted egg proteins and too much heat that squeezes them into curds. Heat gently, stir constantly, stop at about 82°C and cool it quickly — or add starch if you want a custard that can take a boil.
