Slice an apple and within minutes the cut surfaces start to turn tan, then brown. The same thing happens to pears, bananas, avocados, potatoes and mushrooms. It is one of the most familiar reactions in the kitchen, and one of the easiest to control once you know what's driving it.

## The three ingredients of browning

Inside an intact apple, everything needed for browning is present but kept apart in different parts of each cell:

1. **Phenolic compounds** — natural antioxidants and flavour compounds stored mainly in the cell's central compartment, the vacuole.
2. **Polyphenol oxidase (PPO)** — an enzyme, sitting in other structures within the cell. PPO contains copper at its active centre.
3. **Oxygen** — from the air.

When you cut or bruise the fruit, cells are broken and their contents mix. PPO uses oxygen to convert the phenolic compounds into highly reactive molecules called **quinones**. The quinones then react with each other and with other substances to form brown pigments, a family of compounds related to the melanin that colours skin and hair.

Because it needs an enzyme, this is called **enzymatic browning** — unlike the browning of toast or seared meat, which is driven by heat (see [the Maillard reaction vs caramelisation](/maillard-reaction-vs-caramelisation/)).

## Why some apples brown faster

Varieties differ a lot. The speed of browning depends on how much PPO and how many phenolic compounds the fruit contains, and on its natural acidity — all of which vary with variety, ripeness and growing conditions. Food scientists have also developed non-browning apples by switching off the genes responsible for PPO, which shows how central that one enzyme is.

## What stops browning — and why

Every effective method works by removing one of the three ingredients or slowing the enzyme down.

**1. Acid.** PPO works best at a mildly acidic to neutral pH. Lemon juice (around pH 2) pushes the cut surface well below that, slowing the enzyme dramatically. Lime juice and other acidic juices work in the same way, though they also flavour the fruit.

**2. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid).** Lemon juice has a second weapon. Ascorbic acid is a reducing agent: it converts the quinones back to their colourless original form before they can join up into brown pigments. It works until it is used up, which is why browning eventually starts even on lemon-treated fruit. Pure ascorbic acid powder (sold for home preserving) is very effective and adds less flavour than lemon juice.

**3. Cold.** Like most reactions, enzymatic browning slows as temperature drops. Refrigerated cut fruit browns much more slowly than fruit left on the bench.

**4. Keeping oxygen out.** Submerge cut fruit in water, press plastic film directly onto the cut surface of an avocado or apple, or pack slices tightly in a sealed container. Less oxygen means less browning.

**5. Salt water.** A brief dip in lightly salted water — about half a teaspoon of salt per cup of water — slows browning, because chloride ions inhibit PPO. Rinse the fruit afterwards if you don't want any salty taste.

**6. Heat.** Cooking destroys the enzyme. That is why baked apples, poached pears and blanched potatoes don't brown in the same way, and why fruit for freezing is often blanched or packed in syrup.

:::tip The fruit-platter method
For apple slices that need to stay pale for a few hours, toss them in a mix of water and lemon juice (roughly one part juice to four parts water) or a dilute ascorbic acid solution, drain, then refrigerate in a sealed container. It works better than lemon juice alone and the flavour is milder.
:::

## Methods that don't help much

**Sugar on its own** has limited effect, though a heavy syrup does slow browning by limiting oxygen contact. **Oil or butter** can coat a surface but is messy and inconsistent. And leaving the pit in guacamole only protects the small area it covers — pressing film onto the surface is far more effective.

## Is browned fruit safe?

Yes. Enzymatic browning is a cosmetic and flavour change, not spoilage. Browned apple can taste slightly flatter or more bitter, and its texture may soften, but it isn't harmful. Mould, a fermented smell or a slimy texture are different matters — those mean the fruit has spoiled.

## When browning is a good thing

The same enzyme family is welcome elsewhere. The colour and much of the flavour of black tea comes from leaves being deliberately bruised and left to oxidise, letting their own oxidase enzymes go to work. Dates, raisins and some fermented foods also owe part of their colour to enzymatic browning.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Prep kitchens deal with browning all day — apples for salads and garnishes, potatoes peeled ahead, artichokes trimmed in bulk. The standard approaches are the ones above: cut as close to service as possible, hold potatoes and artichokes submerged in water (often acidulated with lemon), keep apple garnishes in acidulated water in the coolroom, and press film directly onto avocado preparations.
:::

## Common questions

### Why do bananas turn black in the fridge?

Bananas are tropical fruit and suffer "chilling injury" at fridge temperatures. The cold damages cells in the peel, which lets their browning enzymes reach the phenolic compounds, so the skin darkens quickly. The flesh inside is often still fine to eat, especially if the banana was ripe before it went in.

### How do packaged apple slices stay white for days?

Many commercial apple slices are dipped in a solution of calcium ascorbate — vitamin C combined with calcium. The ascorbate stops browning in the same way as lemon juice, and the calcium helps keep the slices crisp. The slices are then packed in sealed bags with little air and kept cold.

### Does browning destroy the nutrients in an apple?

Browning uses up some of the apple's phenolic compounds and vitamin C at the cut surface, but the effect on a slice eaten within a few hours is small. It is mainly a change in appearance and flavour.

## The takeaway

Browning happens when cutting lets an enzyme combine the fruit's phenolic compounds with oxygen. Slow the enzyme with acid and cold, reverse the first step with vitamin C, keep air away, or destroy the enzyme with heat — and your cut fruit will stay pale far longer.
