If you have ever cooked eggs straight from a farm or a backyard flock, you know the frustration: you crack the shell and half the white comes away with it, leaving a cratered egg that looks like the surface of the moon. Supermarket eggs, which are usually older, often peel much more cleanly. That isn't your imagination, and it isn't your technique. It is chemistry.

## The layers between shell and white

An egg has more structure than it seems. Inside the hard shell are **two thin membranes** made of protein fibres. The outer one is attached to the shell; the inner one wraps the white. At the wide end of the egg the two membranes separate to form the **air cell**, which grows as the egg ages and loses moisture.

When an egg is cooked, the outer layer of white sets against that inner membrane. Whether the white lets go of the membrane cleanly — or tears — decides how easily the egg peels.

## Why pH is the key

A freshly laid egg contains a fair amount of dissolved **carbon dioxide**, which forms a weak acid in the white. That is why the white of a very fresh egg can look slightly cloudy. At this stage the white has a pH of roughly 7.6–7.9.

Eggshells are porous — thousands of tiny pores let gases pass in and out. Over the days after laying, carbon dioxide escapes, and the white becomes steadily more alkaline. Within about a week or so of refrigerated storage, its pH can climb above 9.

That change matters because the proteins of the white behave differently at different pH levels. At the lower pH of a fresh egg, the white proteins bond strongly to the membrane as they set, so they stick to the shell. At the higher pH of an older egg, the bond is much weaker, and the cooked white releases the membrane more readily.

Sources differ on the exact number — figures quoted range from about pH 8.6 to 9.2 — but the pattern is consistent: once the white has climbed into that range, eggs peel far more cleanly than very fresh ones.

## What actually helps

**1. Use eggs that are not super-fresh.** If you have a choice, keep the freshest eggs for frying and poaching — where a firm, compact white is an advantage — and use eggs that have spent about a week in the fridge for boiling.

**2. Start in boiling water or steam, not cold water.** Lowering eggs into already-boiling water (or a steamer) sets the outermost layer of white very quickly. In tests by many cooks, this "hot start" gives noticeably cleaner peeling than starting eggs in cold water and heating them slowly, which gives the white more time to bond to the membrane as it sets. A hot start also makes cooking times far more consistent.

**3. Chill quickly in iced water.** Moving eggs straight into iced water stops the cooking and causes the egg to contract slightly from the shell. It also makes them comfortable to handle sooner.

**4. Peel from the wide end, under water.** Crack the egg all over, start at the wide end where the air cell gives you a gap, and get your thumb under the inner membrane — not just the shell. Peeling under a gentle stream of water or in a bowl of water helps lift the membrane away.

## Tricks with mixed results

**Baking soda in the water.** Adding bicarbonate of soda makes the cooking water more alkaline, and some cooks find it helps. The effect on the inside of the egg is limited, though, because the shell slows how much reaches the white, and too much can leave a soapy or sulfurous taste. It is a minor helper at best.

**Vinegar in the water.** Vinegar softens the shell slightly and helps any white that leaks from a cracked egg set faster, which is useful, but because it is acidic it does not raise the white's pH. It won't make a fresh egg peel more easily.

**Pricking the shell.** Making a small hole in the wide end lets air escape from the air cell and can reduce cracking, but evidence that it helps peeling is weak.

:::tip The steam method
Put 2–3 cm of water in a saucepan, bring it to a boil, add a steamer basket and the eggs straight from the fridge, cover, and steam for 11–13 minutes for a set yolk (6–7 minutes for soft). Steam cooks almost as fast as boiling water, is gentler on the shells, and the eggs peel well. Then chill them in iced water.
:::

## What about freshness tests?

The float test — placing an egg in water — gives a rough indication of age, because the air cell enlarges as the egg loses moisture. A fresh egg lies on its side at the bottom; an older one tilts or stands on its end. An egg that floats has a very large air cell and is old. The float test tells you about age, not safety, so use your nose and the best-before date as well.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Kitchens that peel large numbers of eggs usually buy them a few days ahead and rotate stock so that eggs for boiling have had time to age a little in the coolroom, while the freshest go to poaching and frying. Many prep cooks peel under running water in a deep container, which speeds up the job and keeps shell fragments out of the eggs.
:::

## The takeaway

Fresh eggs stick because their whites are less alkaline, and that makes the cooked white grip the membrane. Give eggs a few days, start them hot, chill them fast and peel from the wide end, and you'll spend far less time picking shell off your eggs.
