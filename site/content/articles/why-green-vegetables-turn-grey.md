Drop green beans into boiling water and something lovely happens: within a minute they turn a vivid, almost glowing green. Leave them a few minutes longer and that colour slides into a dull olive. The same happens to broccoli, peas, asparagus and spinach. Both changes have clear causes, and once you know them, keeping vegetables bright is easy.

## Why they get brighter first

Raw green vegetables are slightly muted because their tissues contain tiny pockets of air between the cells. That air scatters light and veils the green pigment underneath.

When the vegetable hits boiling water, the air expands and escapes, and water or cell fluids take its place. Without the scattering air, you see the green pigment much more clearly. That vivid green is the sign that the surface has heated through — and a cue that you are near the end of cooking, not the beginning.

## Why they turn olive

The green comes from **chlorophyll**, which plants use to capture light. There are two main forms: chlorophyll *a*, which is blue-green, and chlorophyll *b*, which is yellow-green. Each chlorophyll molecule has a **magnesium** atom at its centre, and that magnesium is what gives it its bright colour.

Plant cells contain natural acids, kept in separate compartments. As cooking breaks down the cell structure — above roughly 60°C the membranes around the chloroplasts are damaged — those acids are released and come into contact with the chlorophyll. Hydrogen ions from the acids push the magnesium out of the centre of the molecule. Without its magnesium, chlorophyll *a* becomes grey-green **pheophytin** *a* and chlorophyll *b* becomes olive-yellow **pheophytin** *b*. The vegetable turns drab.

Anything that brings more acid to the chlorophyll, or gives the reaction more time, makes the colour change worse.

## What makes the colour fade faster

**Time.** This is the biggest factor. The longer vegetables cook, the more chlorophyll is converted. Most green vegetables keep good colour for the first five to seven minutes and then fade quickly.

**Acid.** Adding vinegar or lemon juice during cooking, or cooking green vegetables in an acidic sauce such as tomato, speeds the change dramatically.

**A covered pot.** Some of the acids released from vegetables are volatile. With a lid on, they condense and drip back into the water; uncovered, more of them escape.

**Holding hot.** Vegetables kept warm in a bain-marie or a covered dish keep cooking in their own heat and acids, and lose their colour long after they leave the pot.

**Boiling compared with other methods.** Research comparing cooking methods for vegetables such as green beans, broccoli, peas and spinach found that boiling produced the most pheophytin and microwaving the least, although the result varied by vegetable.

## How to keep green vegetables bright

1. **Use plenty of well-salted boiling water.** A large volume of water returns to the boil quickly after the vegetables go in, so they cook faster, and it dilutes the acids they release. Salt seasons the vegetables; it doesn't meaningfully change their colour.
2. **Cook uncovered and cook briefly.** Cook until just tender — often two to four minutes for beans, broccoli florets or asparagus — and taste a piece.
3. **Shock in iced water.** Drain and plunge into a large bowl of iced water to stop the cooking instantly. Once cold, drain well. The vegetables will hold their colour for hours in the fridge.
4. **Reheat quickly to order.** Toss in a hot pan with butter or oil, or dip back into boiling water for 30 seconds, just before serving.
5. **Dress with acid at the last minute.** Lemon juice and vinaigrettes are delicious on green vegetables, but add them just before they reach the table.

## What about baking soda?

A pinch of bicarbonate of soda in the cooking water makes it alkaline, neutralising the acids and keeping vegetables startlingly green. The trade-offs are serious, though: alkaline water breaks down the cell walls so vegetables turn mushy quickly, it destroys some vitamins such as vitamin C and thiamine, and too much leaves a soapy taste. Most chefs don't use it.

Historically, cooks also kept vegetables green by cooking them in copper pots or even adding copper salts. Copper can take the place of magnesium in chlorophyll and form a very stable green pigment. That is no longer recommended, because too much copper is toxic.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Nearly every restaurant blanches and refreshes its green vegetables: they are cooked in a large pot of heavily salted boiling water during prep, plunged straight into iced water, drained and stored cold. At service they're reheated in seconds with butter or stock. Holding green vegetables hot through a long service is the quickest way to turn them grey, which is why cooks avoid it.
:::

## Common questions

### Why do frozen peas stay so green?

Vegetables for freezing are blanched first, usually within hours of harvest. Blanching inactivates enzymes that would otherwise degrade colour and flavour during storage, and the short cooking time leaves most of the chlorophyll intact. Cook frozen green vegetables briefly — they're already partly cooked.

### Can I cook green vegetables ahead for a dinner party?

Yes, and it's the restaurant method. Blanch and refresh them up to a day ahead, drain them well, and store them in a covered container in the fridge with a sheet of paper towel to absorb moisture. Reheat in a hot pan or boiling water for less than a minute just before serving.

### Why do greens in curries and stews always turn olive?

Long cooking plus acidic ingredients — tomatoes, wine, tamarind, vinegar — is the worst combination for chlorophyll. If you want bright greens in a slow-cooked dish, add them in the last few minutes, or blanch them separately and stir them through at the end.

## The takeaway

Green vegetables turn olive because acids released during cooking knock magnesium out of chlorophyll. Cook them fast in lots of boiling water with the lid off, chill them in iced water, reheat at the last moment, and keep acids away until serving.
