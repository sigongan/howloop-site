Onions are the base of half the world's cooking, and they fight back every time we cut them. The sting and the tears aren't random — they come from a chemical defence system the onion keeps primed and waiting for anything that damages its cells.

## A defence system in two parts

Like garlic, leeks and chives, onions belong to the *Allium* family, and all of them store sulfur-containing compounds in their cells. In an intact onion those compounds are kept separate from the enzymes that act on them.

When you cut or crush an onion, the cells rupture and the contents mix. A chain reaction follows:

1. An enzyme called **alliinase** acts on the onion's main sulfur compound (a sulfoxide derived from the amino acid cysteine), breaking it down into reactive intermediates called sulfenic acids.
2. A second enzyme, **lachrymatory-factor synthase**, quickly converts one of those intermediates into **syn-propanethial-S-oxide** — the "lachrymatory factor", from the Latin for tears.

For decades, scientists assumed the tear-inducing compound formed spontaneously after the first step. In 2002 a team at the Japanese food company House Foods, led by Shinsuke Imai, showed in the journal *Nature* that it is actually made by this second, previously unknown enzyme. The research later won an Ig Nobel Prize — but it also opened the door to onions bred to make little or no lachrymatory factor while keeping their flavour.

## Why it makes your eyes water

Syn-propanethial-S-oxide is volatile, so it rises from the cut surface as a gas. When it reaches your eyes, it irritates the sensory nerve endings in the cornea. Your body responds with a reflex: tears flow to dilute and wash away the irritant. The effect starts within seconds of cutting and peaks soon after, as the enzyme reaction is fast.

## Why garlic doesn't do the same

Garlic has alliinase, but it lacks lachrymatory-factor synthase. Instead, its sulfenic acids combine to form **allicin**, which gives raw garlic its pungent bite. Plenty of sting on the tongue — no tears.

## What actually reduces the tears

The goal is to make less of the gas, keep it from vaporising, or keep it away from your eyes.

**1. Use a sharp knife.** A sharp blade slices cleanly through cells instead of crushing them, so fewer cells rupture and less of the reaction takes place. This is the single most useful habit, and it makes every other cutting job easier too.

**2. Chill the onion.** Twenty to thirty minutes in the fridge (or a few minutes in the freezer) slows the enzymes and makes the gas less volatile, so less of it reaches your eyes. Don't freeze onions solid if you want crisp raw texture.

**3. Move the air.** Cutting near a range hood running on high, an open window or a small fan blows the gas away from your face.

**4. Keep your distance.** Stand up straight with the board slightly further away, rather than leaning over it.

**5. Work fast and cover the cut pieces.** Once diced, move onions into a covered container or straight into the pan so they stop releasing gas into the room.

**6. Protect your eyes.** Well-sealed goggles work completely, if you don't mind how they look. Ordinary glasses help a little.

## Tricks that don't help much

Holding bread or a spoon in your mouth, burning a candle nearby or chewing gum are popular, but there's little good evidence that they do much. Cutting onions under running water does work, because the gas dissolves in the water before reaching your eyes — but it's awkward and unsafe with a sharp knife, so it isn't worth it.

Many cooks also leave the **root end** until last, believing it releases more irritant. It's a sensible habit, as keeping the root intact also holds the layers together for neat dicing, but the evidence on how much difference it makes to tears is thin.

## Why cooked onions taste sweet

Heat destroys the enzymes and drives off the volatile sulfur compounds. That is why cooked onions don't sting and why slowly cooked onions taste mellow and sweet: the harsh compounds are gone, and the onion's natural sugars, plus the browning reactions that follow, take over. (See [the Maillard reaction vs caramelisation](/maillard-reaction-vs-caramelisation/) for what happens next.)

It is also why raw onions lose some of their bite if they are soaked in cold water for 10–15 minutes after slicing: some of the pungent compounds dissolve away.

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Cooks who dice kilos of onions a day rely on the basics rather than tricks: a very sharp knife, onions kept in the coolroom, a cutting station under good extraction, and covered containers for the diced onion. A sharp knife and efficient technique — fewer cuts, cleaner slices — do more than anything else.
:::

## Common questions

### Why do some onions make me cry more than others?

Pungency varies with variety and growing conditions. The sulfur compounds behind the sting depend partly on how much sulfur the plant takes up from the soil, which is why mild "sweet" onions are often grown in low-sulfur soils. Brown storage onions tend to be the most pungent; many red and sweet onions are gentler.

### Why does peeling an onion not make me cry, but cutting does?

Removing the papery outer skin breaks very few living cells. Slicing through the fleshy layers ruptures thousands of them, mixing the sulfur compounds with the enzymes and starting the reaction. The more finely you chop, the more cells you break — which is why mincing is worse than slicing.

### Do contact lenses or glasses help?

They can help a little by shielding part of the eye's surface from the gas, but only well-sealed goggles block it reliably.

## The takeaway

Onion tears come from a fast, two-enzyme reaction that turns sulfur compounds into an irritating gas when the onion's cells are broken. Cut cleanly with a sharp knife, chill the onions, keep the air moving and keep your face back — and remember that cooking turns the same onion sweet and gentle.
