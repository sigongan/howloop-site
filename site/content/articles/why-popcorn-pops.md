Popcorn is one of the few foods that cooks itself in a single, spectacular moment. A hard, glassy kernel sits in hot oil for a minute or two, then — in a few hundredths of a second — bursts, flips into the air and expands to many times its size. It's a neat bit of physics, and it explains why popcorn is so particular about how it's stored.

## A kernel built like a pressure vessel

A popcorn kernel has three main parts:

- the **pericarp**, or hull — a hard, tough outer layer;
- the **endosperm** — mostly starch, packed tightly in a hard, glassy form with a small amount of softer starch; and
- the **germ** — the embryo of the plant.

What makes popcorn special is the pericarp. In popcorn it is unusually strong and much less permeable to water vapour than in other types of maize. That means the kernel can hold in steam as it heats rather than letting it leak away. Other kinds of corn, with weaker or leakier hulls, just scorch.

Inside, the endosperm holds a small amount of water — ideally about **13.5–14% of the kernel's weight**, according to the industry body The Popcorn Board.

## Building the pressure

As the kernel heats past 100°C, its water turns to steam. Trapped by the hull, the steam can't escape, so pressure rises — just as in a pressure cooker. The hot, pressurised steam also softens the starch around it, turning it into a hot, molten, gelatinised mass.

Classic research by Hoseney and colleagues in 1983 found that popping happens at about **177°C**, corresponding to an internal pressure of around **135 pounds per square inch** — roughly nine times atmospheric pressure.

## The pop

When the pressure becomes more than the hull can bear, the pericarp ruptures. The pressure drops instantly, and the superheated water inside flashes into steam, expanding enormously. The softened starch is blown outwards into a foam of thin-walled bubbles that cools and sets almost immediately into the crisp, white flake we eat.

In 2015 two French physicists, Emmanuel Virot and Alexandre Ponomarenko, filmed popping kernels at 2,900 frames per second and published their analysis in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*. They found:

- **A sharp threshold.** Only about a third of kernels popped when heated to 170°C, but 96% popped at 180°C — consistent with a pressure-vessel model, regardless of kernel size or shape.
- **The jump.** A kernel jumps because a "leg" of expanding starch pushes against the pan, flipping the popcorn into the air like an acrobat — from a few millimetres to several centimetres high.
- **The sound.** The "pop" is not the hull cracking but the sudden release of water vapour from the kernel.

## Why some kernels don't pop

The unpopped kernels at the bottom of the bowl — sometimes called "old maids" — usually fail for one of two reasons:

1. **Too little moisture.** Kernels that have dried out don't have enough water to build the pressure needed to burst the hull and puff the starch. This is the most common cause in kernels that have been stored open for a long time.
2. **A damaged hull.** A crack or weak spot lets steam leak out gradually, so pressure never builds to the bursting point.

Too much moisture is also a problem: it can weaken the hull so it fails early, giving small, chewy popcorn. That's why popcorn is dried to a narrow moisture range and sold in sealed containers.

## How to pop better popcorn

1. **Store kernels airtight.** Keep them in a sealed jar at room temperature. The fridge and freezer can dry kernels out over time.
2. **Heat the oil properly first.** Add three or four kernels to the oil and wait until they pop; then add the rest in a single layer. Starting hot gets every kernel through the threshold quickly and evenly.
3. **Let the steam out.** Leave the lid slightly ajar or use a vented lid. Steam escaping from the pan keeps the popcorn crisp rather than soggy.
4. **Shake and stop on time.** Shake the pan to keep kernels moving, and take it off the heat when the pops slow to a few seconds apart, before the bottom layer scorches.
5. **Season while hot.** Fine salt sticks best; very finely ground salt is sold specifically for popcorn. A light spray of oil or melted butter helps seasonings cling.

:::tip Two shapes of popcorn
Popcorn varieties pop into two main shapes. **Butterfly** (or snowflake) popcorn has irregular "wings" — light and tender, typical of cinema popcorn. **Mushroom** popcorn pops into round, dense balls that don't break easily, which is why it's preferred for caramel corn and other coatings.
:::

## Other grains that pop

Popcorn is the champion, but it isn't alone. Sorghum pops into small, tender puffs, and amaranth puffs when heated in a dry pan. Rice and other grains can be puffed industrially by heating them under pressure and releasing it suddenly — the same principle, with a machine acting as the hull.

## Common questions

### Can you make popcorn without oil?

Yes. Hot-air poppers and paper bags in the microwave both work, because the kernel only needs to reach its popping temperature. Oil helps transfer heat evenly in a pan and helps seasoning stick. In one comparison published by cereal scientists, hot-air popping actually produced a larger popped volume than oil popping across a range of moisture levels.

### Can old kernels be revived?

Sometimes. Because the usual problem with old kernels is lost moisture, some cooks add a teaspoon or so of water to a sealed jar of kernels, shake well and leave it for a few days so the water is absorbed. Results vary, and it can't fix kernels with damaged hulls.

### Why does popcorn go stale and chewy?

Popped popcorn is a dry, crisp starch foam that readily absorbs moisture from the air. As it takes up water it softens and turns leathery — the opposite of bread, which goes hard as it stales. Store leftover popcorn in an airtight container.

## The takeaway

A popcorn kernel is a sealed pressure vessel: its water turns to steam, pressure builds to about nine atmospheres, and at roughly 180°C the hull gives way and the starch explodes into foam. Keep kernels sealed so they hold their moisture, start with hot oil, let the steam escape, and you'll have fewer duds and crisper popcorn.
