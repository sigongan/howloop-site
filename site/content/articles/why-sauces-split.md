Mayonnaise, hollandaise, béarnaise, beurre blanc, vinaigrette, a glossy butter-finished pan sauce — some of the most important sauces in cooking are mixtures of fat and water that, by rights, shouldn't mix at all. When they hold together, they're creamy and luxurious. When they don't, you get a greasy, curdled mess. The science of emulsions explains both.

## Oil and water don't mix — unless you help them

Oil and water separate because water molecules are strongly attracted to each other and not to oil. Shake them together and you get droplets that quickly merge and float back up.

An **emulsion** is a mixture in which one liquid is broken into tiny droplets dispersed through the other. Food emulsions come in two types:

- **Oil-in-water** — fat droplets in a watery liquid: mayonnaise, hollandaise, vinaigrette, cream, milk.
- **Water-in-oil** — water droplets in fat: butter is the classic example.

To make an emulsion stable, you need two things.

**1. Small droplets.** Whisking or blending breaks the fat into tiny droplets. The smaller and more uniform they are, the more stable and creamy the sauce.

**2. An emulsifier.** Emulsifiers are molecules with one end that likes water and one end that likes fat. They coat each droplet, with their fat-loving end in the oil and their water-loving end facing the water, preventing droplets from merging. Egg yolk is the cook's great emulsifier: it contains **lecithin** and other phospholipids, plus proteins and lipoprotein particles that all do the job. Mustard helps too, as do the proteins and starches in many sauces.

A third helper is **thickness**: in a thick, viscous sauce, droplets move slowly and are less likely to collide and merge.

## Why sauces split

**Too much oil, too fast.** At the start of a mayonnaise, there is only a little watery liquid (from the yolk, lemon juice or vinegar) and lots of emulsifier. If you pour oil in quickly, the whisk can't break it into small droplets fast enough, and large droplets merge into an oily layer. That's why mayonnaise starts drop by drop.

**Too little water.** As you add more oil, the droplets pack tighter and tighter. The sauce gets thicker until there isn't enough watery liquid left between the droplets to keep them apart, and the emulsion collapses. A mayonnaise that has become very stiff and starts to look greasy needs a few drops of water or lemon juice before more oil goes in.

**Too much heat.** Warm emulsions like hollandaise rely on egg-yolk proteins that thicken as they gently heat. Push them too far and the proteins coagulate into tiny scrambled lumps that can no longer stabilise the fat, and the butter leaks out. Hollandaise is usually cooked until the yolks thicken and held warm, never hot.

**Too little heat.** Butter sauces such as beurre blanc need to stay warm enough for the butterfat to stay liquid. Let them cool too far and the fat starts to solidify and separate; heat them too much and the butter's own emulsion breaks into clear fat and milk solids. Beurre blanc is a sauce for a warm spot on the stove, not a hot one.

**Acid, salt or time.** A vinaigrette without an emulsifier separates within minutes because nothing coats the droplets. That's normal — a quick whisk or shake re-forms it.

## How to rescue a broken sauce

The universal fix is to **start a new emulsion and feed the broken one into it**.

**Broken mayonnaise or aioli.** Put a fresh egg yolk (or a teaspoon of mustard, or a tablespoon of water) in a clean bowl. Whisk in the broken mayonnaise a few drops at a time, then in a thin stream as it thickens. It will come back together.

**Split hollandaise or béarnaise.** If it's just starting to look grainy because it's too hot, take it off the heat at once and whisk in a tablespoon of cold water to cool it. If it has fully split, whisk a fresh yolk with a little warm water over gentle heat and whisk the broken sauce back in slowly. If it split because it got too cold and the butter set, warm it very gently while whisking in a little warm water.

**Broken beurre blanc.** Off the heat, whisk a tablespoon of cream or a little of the reduction into a clean pan, then whisk the broken sauce back in gradually.

**Greasy pan sauce.** A splash of water or stock and vigorous whisking (or a quick blitz with a stick blender) usually brings back a glossy finish.

## Tips for sauces that don't split

- Use ingredients at similar temperatures — room-temperature eggs and oil for mayonnaise.
- Add oil or butter gradually, especially at the start.
- Keep a little water or stock handy to adjust thickness before the sauce gets too tight.
- Use a stick blender for mayonnaise: put all the ingredients in a narrow jar, place the blender at the bottom and pull it up slowly. The intense shearing forms an emulsion in seconds.
- Hold warm emulsions in a warm (not hot) place — a bowl over warm water or an insulated container.

:::safety Raw-egg sauces
Mayonnaise, aioli and hollandaise made with raw or lightly cooked eggs have been linked to *Salmonella* outbreaks. Food-safety agencies advise using pasteurised eggs for sauces that won't be thoroughly cooked — especially when serving young children, older people, pregnant people or anyone with a weakened immune system — and refrigerating raw-egg sauces and using them quickly.
:::

:::chef In a professional kitchen
Most restaurant sauce sections make hollandaise and béarnaise in small batches through service and hold them in a warm spot or an insulated container, because a sauce that sits too hot will split and one that sits too cool will set. A cup of warm water and a spare yolk are kept nearby for rescues. Many kitchens also use pasteurised egg yolks for emulsified sauces.
:::

## The takeaway

Emulsified sauces are tiny droplets of fat held apart by emulsifiers and water. They break when the fat goes in too fast, when there's too little water, or when the temperature drifts too high or too low. To fix one, start fresh with a little yolk, mustard or water and slowly whisk the broken sauce back in.
