A radiator is a container of hot water with a fixed volume. When air gets into
the system it does what air always does — it rises to the highest point it can
reach and sits there. In a panel radiator that point is the top few inches, and
water can no longer get to it. The bottom stays hot, the top goes cold, and the
room takes twice as long to warm up.

The fix is to open a small valve at the top and let the air out. It takes about
five minutes and costs nothing. The part people get wrong is not the bleeding —
it's what happens to the boiler afterwards.

## First, check it's actually air

This takes ten seconds and saves you from doing the wrong job entirely.

Run the heating for fifteen minutes, then put a hand on the radiator low down
and then high up.

- **Hot bottom, cold top** → trapped air. Bleeding will fix it.
- **Cold everywhere** → not air. The valve at either end may be closed or
  seized, or the radiator isn't getting flow.
- **Hot top, cold bottom** → sludge sitting in the base. Bleeding does nothing
  for this; it needs a proper flush.

<Callout type="warn" title="Let it cool first — this one matters">
The water in a heating system runs well above the temperature that scalds skin,
and it is under pressure. Turn the heating off and wait until the radiators are
cool enough to hold your hand against comfortably. Bleeding a hot system is how
people end up with burns on their wrists.
</Callout>

## Opening the valve

The bleed valve is the small square or slotted screw at the top of one end of
the radiator. Older ones take a **bleed key** — a cheap stamped-metal square you
can buy anywhere that sells DIY supplies. Many newer valves take a flat-blade
screwdriver instead.

Put a cloth on the floor and hold a bowl or cup directly under the valve. Water
will come out, and it is usually black.

Turn the key **anticlockwise, slowly**. A quarter turn is often enough. You want
a steady hiss, not a rush. Half a turn is the maximum you should ever need.

Do not keep unscrewing to make it go faster. Beyond about a full turn the screw
can come out entirely, and at that point you have a hole in a pressurised
heating system and no way to close it. That is a genuinely bad afternoon.

## Closing at the right moment

Listen. The hiss will thin out and stop, and a dribble of water will appear.
**That is the moment.** All the air is out and water has reached the valve.

Close it right away — turn clockwise until it is snug, then stop. Do not haul on
it. The seat is soft brass and overtightening deforms it, which gives you a
valve that weeps permanently and needs replacing.

Wipe the area dry and check it after a minute. If it is still beading water, it
needs a fraction more. If it drips steadily even when firmly closed, the valve
is worn out.

## The step everyone forgets

You have just removed water from a sealed system. The pressure has dropped.

Look at the gauge on the front of your boiler. Cold, most combi boilers want to
sit around **1 to 1.5 bar** — the green band on the dial. If it has fallen below
that, the boiler may refuse to fire, and you will have "fixed" one radiator and
disabled all of them.

Topping up is done with the **filling loop**, usually a braided silver hose with
a tap at each end, under or beside the boiler. Open both taps briefly, watch the
gauge climb, and close them as soon as you reach the middle of the band. Do it
in short bursts — the gauge moves faster than you expect, and overshooting into
the red means bleeding some back out again.

<Callout type="tip" title="Do the whole house in one go">
If one radiator has air, others probably do too. Work ground floor first, then
upstairs, and within each floor start with the radiator furthest from the
boiler. Then top the pressure up once at the end rather than after every
radiator.
</Callout>

## When it isn't a five-minute job

- **You are bleeding the same radiator every few weeks.** Air is getting in
  continuously — a small leak, a failing pump seal, or internal corrosion making
  hydrogen. Repeated bleeding treats the symptom and accelerates the corrosion.
- **The water that comes out is thick and black.** Heavy sludge. The system
  needs cleaning.
- **The bleed screw is seized or rounded off.** Stop before you shear it.
- **Pressure drops on its own between top-ups.** There is a leak somewhere.
- **Anything involving the boiler itself.** Gas appliances are not DIY.

Bleeding is one of the few heating jobs that is genuinely safe for anyone to do.
Everything past it isn't, and the line is worth respecting.
