There's a specific moment when people decide their phone is dying: the cable
stops staying in. You wiggle it, you prop it against a book at just the right
angle, you buy a new cable, and eventually you start pricing replacements.

In most cases nothing is broken. The port is full.

Every time you put your phone in a pocket, fibres work their way into the
opening. Every time you plug the cable in, the plug acts like a tiny ram and
compresses those fibres against the back wall. After a year or two of this it
isn't fluff any more — it's a dense felt pad that physically stops the plug
travelling far enough to reach the contacts.

Which is exactly why holding the cable at an angle sometimes works. You're
bridging a gap that shouldn't be there.

## Read the symptom first

This job is worth doing if:

- The plug **doesn't click** the way it used to
- It **sits proud** of the case, or **wobbles**
- It charges **only at a certain angle**, or falls out on its own
- Charging is **intermittent** — starts, stops, starts

It is probably **not** lint if the phone charges perfectly on a wireless pad but
not at all by cable, or if nothing charges from any source. Those point at the
port hardware or the battery.

<Callout type="warn" title="Turn the phone fully off">
Not standby — off. There are live contacts a few millimetres from where you're
about to work, and a short across them can damage the charging circuit. This is
ten seconds of prevention against a repair bill.
</Callout>

## Look before you poke

Hold a torch — a second phone's flashlight is fine — at a shallow angle and look
straight down into the port. Reading glasses or a magnifier help more than you'd
expect.

You're looking for a **flat grey pad at the far end**, often with the shape of
the plug pressed into it. Once you've seen it, everything about the wobbly cable
makes sense.

If instead you see green or white powdery deposits, that's corrosion from
moisture, and it's a different problem — isopropyl alcohol on a cotton bud, or a
repair shop.

## The scrape

**Use a wooden toothpick.** Not a pin, not a paperclip, not a SIM ejector tool,
not tweezers. The contacts in there are thin metal strips a fraction of a
millimetre thick, and metal bends them permanently. Wood is stiff enough to
scrape lint and soft enough that it gives before the contacts do.

Technique matters more than force:

1. Slide the toothpick in **along the top or bottom wall**, not straight down the
   middle where the contacts are.
2. Get **behind** the lint pad — past it, toward the back wall.
3. **Drag outward** in short strokes. You are pulling material out of the
   opening, not pressing it in.
4. Work round the edges as well as the centre.

It comes out in surprising quantity, usually in two or three grey clumps. Most
people are quietly appalled by how much was in there.

Go slowly. There is no version of this where speed helps.

## Finish and test

A **soft dry brush** — a clean toothbrush, a paintbrush — clears the loose dust.
Or a **short burst of compressed air** from a few centimetres away. Keep the can
upright and don't hold the trigger; sustained pressure can push fine debris
further in, and a tilted can can spit liquid propellant.

Then power the phone on and plug the cable in.

**It should click and sit flush**, with no wobble and no holding required. That
click is the whole point — it means the plug reached the bottom of the port for
the first time in months.

## If it still doesn't charge

Work through it in this order, because each step rules something out:

1. **Different cable.** Cables fail far more often than ports, especially at the
   strain point next to the plug.
2. **Different power adapter or socket.**
3. **Charge from a computer's USB port.** Different power path entirely.
4. **Try a wireless charger** if the phone supports it. If wireless works and
   cable doesn't, the port is the fault.

If none of those charge it, the problem is likely the battery or the charging
circuit, and that's a repair shop rather than a toothpick.

## Keeping it clear

The lint comes from your clothes, so it will come back. But now that you know
what to look for, a torch and ten seconds every few months catches it while it's
still loose fluff — which brushes out — rather than a compacted pad, which
doesn't.

A case with a port cover, or a small silicone plug, cuts it down substantially if
you're the sort of person who carries a phone in a coat pocket.
