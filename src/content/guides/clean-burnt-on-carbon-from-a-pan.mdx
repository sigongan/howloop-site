You can scrub a scorched pan with washing-up liquid for twenty minutes and
achieve almost nothing. That is not a failure of effort. It is a failure of
chemistry, and once you know what the black layer actually is, the right method
becomes obvious.

Cooking oil heated in a thin film does not simply burn away. It **polymerises** —
the molecules cross-link into a hard, sticky varnish bonded to the metal. Keep
heating it and that varnish **carbonises** into the black crust you are looking
at. Detergent works by lifting grease. This has not been grease for a long time.

## First: which pan is it?

This is the step that separates cleaning a pan from ruining one. The layer is
the same; what you are allowed to use on it is not.

| Material | Simmer with soap | Baking soda paste | Oxalic cleanser | Abrasive pad |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Stainless steel** | Yes | Yes | Yes | Non-scratch only |
| **Non-stick (PTFE)** | Yes, gently | **No** | **No** | **No** |
| **Bare aluminium** | Yes | **No** — darkens it | Sparingly | Soft only |
| **Anodised aluminium** | Yes | **No** | No | **No** |
| **Cast iron** | Yes | Sparingly | No | Yes, then re-oil |
| **Enamelled cast iron** | Yes | Yes | Gently | Non-scratch only |

Two rows deserve explanation, because they are the ones people get wrong.

**Bare aluminium and baking soda.** Aluminium reacts with alkali. A baking soda
paste will lift the carbon and leave the metal underneath grey, dull and blotchy
— permanently. For aluminium, use an acidic approach instead: cream of tartar or
a splash of vinegar simmered in water.

**Non-stick and anything abrasive.** The coating is only a few microns thick.
Any scratch is permanent, and once it is scratched food sticks in that spot
forever, which produces more burnt-on marks. Soaking is the only safe route.

<Callout type="stop" title="Never use oven cleaner on aluminium">
Oven cleaners are strongly caustic. Caustic soda attacks aluminium violently —
pitting the surface, turning it black, and releasing hydrogen gas. This is a
genuine hazard, not a cosmetic one. Even on stainless, oven cleaner is far more
chemical than this job needs.
</Callout>

## Step one: simmer, do not scrub

Almost everyone starts scrubbing. Start with heat and water instead — it does
most of the work for free.

Cover the burnt area with water, add a squeeze of washing-up liquid, and bring
it to a **gentle simmer for 10 to 15 minutes**. What is happening is that water
is working its way into the crust and heat is loosening the bond to the metal.

Then take a **wooden or plastic scraper** — a spatula edge is fine — and push at
the softened crust. Large flakes should lift. If they do not, simmer for another
ten minutes rather than reaching for something harder.

For the **outside base**, where you cannot hold water, do the equivalent: lay a
soaked cloth over the area for half an hour so the crust never dries out.

<Callout type="warn" title="Never put cold water into a hot pan">
Thermal shock warps pan bases, and a warped base never sits flat on a hob again
— which means uneven heating and more burning. Let the pan cool to warm before
adding water, and never plunge a hot pan into a cold sink.
</Callout>

## Step two: paste, and circles

For stainless steel and enamelled cast iron, make a **thick paste of baking soda
and water** — the consistency of toothpaste, not a slurry. Spread it over the
crust and leave it **15 to 30 minutes**. It is doing two jobs at once: mild
alkali softening the polymer, and fine particles providing gentle abrasion.

Then scrub with a **non-scratch pad, in small circles**. This is not a detail.
The crust has grain and edges; circular motion attacks it from every direction
and gets under the flakes. Straight back-and-forth strokes tend to burnish the
surface rather than cut it.

**Repeat rather than press harder.** Three gentle rounds remove more than one
aggressive round, and they do not scratch the pan.

## Step three: the last brown shadow

You will often reach a point where the crust is gone but a faint brown or
rainbow stain remains. That is **discolouration, not deposit**, and baking soda
will not touch it.

An **oxalic-acid cleanser** (Bar Keepers Friend and equivalents) will. Make a
thin paste, give it a minute or two of contact, scrub briefly, and **rinse
thoroughly**. Keep contact time short, wear gloves, and never use it on
non-stick or on unsealed aluminium.

If you would rather not use it, the stain is purely cosmetic and does not affect
cooking at all.

## Two myths worth dropping

**Vinegar and baking soda together.** They neutralise each other. The fizz is
carbon dioxide being released as an acid and a base destroy one another, leaving
you with salty water. Both are useful cleaners — separately.

**"Boil a dishwasher tablet in it."** This works to a degree, because dishwasher
detergent is strongly alkaline. It is also strongly alkaline on *you*, and it
will damage aluminium and dull some finishes. Baking soda achieves most of the
same effect with none of the risk.

## Stopping it coming back

The black ring around the base of a pan is almost always oil that ran over the
rim and got baked on at the next cooking session. Three habits stop it:

1. **Lower heat.** Polymerisation accelerates sharply once oil passes its smoke
   point. Most home cooking runs hotter than it needs to.
2. **Wipe the outside while the pan is still warm.** Ten seconds then, versus
   thirty minutes in three months.
3. **Use less oil.** A thin film that actually gets used is fine. Oil creeping up
   the sides is the raw material for the next crust.

And when the pan is dry, a **very thin** wipe of oil on bare steel or cast iron
protects it. Thin is the operative word — a thick coat is precisely what
polymerises into the layer you just spent half an hour removing.
