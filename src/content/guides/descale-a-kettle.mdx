Tap water in a hard-water area carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. When you
boil it, some of that water leaves as steam and the minerals it was carrying
have nowhere to go, so they come out of solution and deposit on the hottest
surface — the element. Over months it builds into a crust.

That crust is a genuinely good insulator. The element now has to heat through a
layer of rock before it can heat the water, so it stays on longer, uses more
electricity, and eventually runs hot enough to fail early. A furred kettle can
take noticeably longer to boil than a clean one.

The good news is that limescale dissolves in weak acid, and you almost certainly
have some in the cupboard.

## The mix

**White vinegar and water, roughly half and half.** Enough to cover the scale,
never above the maximum fill line.

That's it. Commercial kettle descalers are mostly citric or sulfamic acid at a
similar working strength, sold at several times the price.

**Citric acid** is the upgrade if you want one. Two tablespoons of the powder in
a kettle of water works faster than vinegar and leaves no smell at all — which,
if you've ever descaled with vinegar on a warm day, is worth something. It's
sold cheaply as a baking or preserving ingredient.

**Lemon juice** works too, on the same chemistry, but you need a lot of lemons to
match a splash of vinegar.

<Callout type="warn" title="Don't improvise the acid">
Stick to food-grade acids — vinegar, citric acid, lemon. Never use descalers
meant for toilets, drains or masonry in something you drink from. They're
formulated to be much more aggressive and to leave residues that are not
supposed to end up in tea.
</Callout>

## Boil once, then wait

Bring it to the boil, switch it off, and **walk away for about thirty minutes.**

The heat gets the reaction going quickly; the soak is what actually does the
work. You may hear faint fizzing — that's carbon dioxide coming off as the acid
converts the carbonate. It's the reaction working.

Re-boiling it repeatedly doesn't speed anything up and just wastes electricity.
Once, then wait.

For a badly furred kettle, leave it an hour or two, or overnight. Time costs
nothing.

## Pouring out and wiping

Tip the solution away and look inside. The crust should be soft, loose, or gone
entirely. Wipe the base and lower walls with a soft sponge or cloth and it comes
away.

**Do not scrape.** No wire wool, no scourers, no knives. Scratching the element
creates a rougher surface, and rough surfaces give new scale far more to grip
onto — so an aggressive clean today means faster rebuild for the rest of the
kettle's life. If it doesn't wipe off easily, it needs another soak, not more
force.

## The rinse matters

**Two full boils of fresh water, poured away each time.** Then a cold rinse.

Skip this and your first cup of tea tastes like a chip shop. Two boils reliably
clears it; one usually doesn't.

<Callout type="tip" title="Doing it early is much less work">
A thin film of scale lifts off in a single thirty-minute soak. A thick crust
takes three rounds and still doesn't fully clear. Look inside your kettle once a
month — the moment you can see white forming on the element, deal with it. It's
the same job either way, just shorter.
</Callout>

## Keeping it clear

- **Only boil what you need.** Less water boiled means less scale deposited. It
  also saves electricity, which is the point at which this stops being about the
  kettle.
- **Empty it after use.** Water left standing overnight deposits minerals as it
  cools.
- **A stainless steel descaling ball** sitting in the kettle attracts scale onto
  itself instead of the element. They're a few pounds and genuinely work.
- **A filter jug** reduces hardness before the water ever reaches the kettle,
  which helps the coffee machine and the iron too.

None of these stop scale entirely in a hard-water area. They just stretch the
gap between descales from one month to three.
