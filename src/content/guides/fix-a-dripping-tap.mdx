There is a specific difference between a tap that drips and a tap that leaks,
and it decides the whole job.

A tap that **leaks around the handle while running** has a worn gland or O-ring
higher up. A tap that **drips from the spout when it is fully closed** has
failed at the point where it is supposed to seal — a rubber washer pressing down
onto a brass ring called the seat, right at the bottom of the tap body.

That second one is this guide. It is the common one, it wastes water
continuously, and it costs pennies to fix.

## First: which kind of tap do you have?

This matters more than anything else here, because the two types share almost
nothing.

- **Compression tap** — the handle **turns several times** from off to fully
  open, and it gets stiffer as you close it. Inside is a rubber washer. This
  guide.
- **Lever mixer / quarter-turn tap** — the handle moves **90 degrees** and feels
  the same the whole way. Inside is a ceramic disc cartridge. The fix is to swap
  the cartridge, not a washer.

If you have to think about it, turn the tap on and count the rotations. Several
turns means a washer.

<Callout type="warn" title="Stop tightening it">
The instinct when a tap drips is to close it harder. Every time you do, you
crush a perished washer further into the brass seat and cut a groove into it.
That turns a washer job into a reseating job. If it drips, leave it alone until
you can fix it.
</Callout>

## Turning the water off properly

Look under the basin at the pipes feeding the tap. Most have a small **isolation
valve** — a brass fitting with a screwdriver slot. Turn it so the slot lies
**across** the pipe: that is closed.

No isolation valve? You need the **main stop tap**. Common locations are under
the kitchen sink, in an airing cupboard, under the stairs, or in a hallway near
the front door. Find it and check it turns *before* you take a tap apart. A stop
tap that has not moved in fifteen years sometimes does not want to.

Then **open the tap** to drain the pipe, and **put the plug in the basin**. That
second one is not fussiness — the screw inside a tap handle is small, and an
open plughole is exactly the right size for it.

## Getting the head out

Four moves, in order:

1. **The cap.** The red or blue disc on top of the handle prises off with a
   small flat screwdriver or a fingernail. Some screw off instead — try turning
   before you lever.
2. **The screw.** Underneath the cap, usually cross-head. It can be long. Put it
   somewhere it cannot roll.
3. **The handle.** Lifts straight up. If it is stuck with limescale, wrap it in
   a cloth soaked in warm water for a few minutes, or gently rock rather than
   yank.
4. **The headgear nut.** The large hexagonal nut on the tap body. Loosen it with
   an adjustable spanner, then unscrew the whole assembly out by hand.

<Callout type="warn" title="Hold the tap body while you turn the nut">
The headgear nut is often tight, and all your force goes into twisting the tap
against the basin. Steady the body with a second spanner or a gloved hand. This
is how cracked basins and snapped tap tails happen — the repair costs a hundred
times what the washer does.
</Callout>

## The washer, and the part people skip

At the bottom of what you have just removed is the **jumper** — a small brass
disc — with the **washer** attached under it by a tiny nut. Undo the nut, take
the old washer off, and fit the new one. Take the old one with you when you buy
replacements so the diameter matches.

A perished washer looks obviously wrong once it is out: hard, cracked, grooved,
or squashed out of round.

**Now look down into the tap body with a torch.** You are looking at the
**seat** — the brass ring the washer lands on. Run a fingertip over it (carefully;
old brass can have burrs).

- **Smooth** → refit and you are done.
- **Scored, pitted or rough** → a new washer will seal for a few weeks and then
  start dripping again, and you will conclude the repair failed. It did not; the
  surface is damaged.

Two fixes for a bad seat, both cheap:

- **A reseating tool** — a hand tool that screws into the tap body and cuts the
  brass flat again. A few pounds, one minute of turning.
- **Nylon seating washers** — a dome of nylon that sits over the damaged seat and
  moulds to it. Even cheaper and requires no tool, though it is a patch rather
  than a repair.

## Back together

Screw the head assembly back in and tighten the headgear nut **firmly but not
brutally** — snug plus a small nudge. Overtightening distorts the seal and makes
the tap stiff for the rest of its life.

Refit the handle, the screw and the cap. Check the handle is in the closed
position before you turn the water on, or you will get a surprise.

Open the isolation valve **slowly**. Run the tap for thirty seconds to push the
air out — it will splutter, which is normal. Then close it and watch the spout
for a full minute.

## When to call a plumber

- The tap body turns with the nut, or the basin moves.
- Water appears **under** the basin rather than in it.
- The stop tap will not close, or drips once you have used it.
- The tap tail or the pipe below it is corroded green and crumbly.
- It is a mixer with a leaking body rather than a dripping spout.

None of those are washer problems, and all of them get more expensive the longer
you improvise.
