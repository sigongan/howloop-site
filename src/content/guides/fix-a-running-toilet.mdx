There are only two ways a toilet can run forever, and once you can picture the
inside of the tank you can tell them apart in about ninety seconds.

Either **water is leaking out of the tank into the bowl**, so the tank never
stays full and the fill valve keeps topping it up — or **the tank is being
overfilled**, so water pours into the overflow tube and down the bowl
continuously. The first is a flapper problem. The second is a float problem.
Everything else is detail.

## The four parts you need to recognise

Lift the lid and ignore everything except these:

- **The flapper.** A rubber flap at the bottom of the tank covering a hole about
  the size of a fist. When you flush, it lifts and the tank empties. It is the
  only thing standing between the tank and the bowl.
- **The chain.** Links the flush handle to the flapper. Length is everything.
- **The overflow tube.** The tall open pipe in the middle. Its job is to send
  water safely into the bowl if the tank overfills, instead of onto your floor.
  Its top edge is also your target water level.
- **The fill valve and float.** The tall column at one side, with either a
  cup-shaped float around it or an arm with a ball on the end. It refills the
  tank and shuts off when the float rises far enough.

<Callout type="tip" title="Put the lid somewhere safe first">
Tank lids are heavy porcelain and they break on impact. Lay it flat on a folded
towel on the floor, not balanced on the seat or leaned against the wall.
</Callout>

## The dye test does the diagnosis for you

This is the step people skip and then spend an hour guessing.

Add ten drops of food colouring to the water in the **tank** — not the bowl.
Then do nothing for twenty minutes. No flushing, no using the toilet.

- **Colour appears in the bowl** → the flapper is leaking. Go to the flapper
  section below.
- **The bowl stays clear** → the flapper is fine. The tank is overfilling. Go to
  the float section.

That's it. One test, two possible answers, no dismantling.

## If it's the flapper

Turn the water off at the shut-off valve behind the toilet, flush to empty the
tank, and look at the flapper closely.

**Check the chain first, because it's free.** Lift the flapper and let it drop.
It should fall flat and stay there. Two common failures:

- **Too tight.** The chain is holding the flapper a millimetre off its seat.
  You often cannot see the gap, but water finds it. Move the clip one or two
  links along.
- **Too long.** The slack chain slides under the flapper as it closes and props
  it open. Same fix, other direction. Aim for around 12 mm of slack.

**Then check the seal.** Run a finger around the plastic seat the flapper lands
on. Grit, mineral scale or a stray hair will hold it open. Wipe it clean with a
cloth.

**Then check the rubber itself.** Press the flapper with a gloved finger. Good
rubber is springy. If it feels hard, or if it leaves black marks on your glove,
it has perished and no amount of adjustment will save it. Replacements are a
few pounds, take about five minutes, and require no tools — unhook the old one,
hook the new one on, reattach the chain.

<Callout type="warn" title="Buy the matching flapper, not the nearest one">
Flappers are not universal. Diameter and hinge style vary between
manufacturers, and a nearly-right one will seat unevenly and leak from day one.
Take the old flapper with you, or photograph the brand name stamped inside the
tank.
</Callout>

## If it's the float

Look at where the water settles. It should sit roughly **25 mm below the top of
the overflow tube**. If it is level with the tube's rim, water is quietly
pouring in and the fill valve can never reach its shut-off point.

How you lower it depends on the age of the toilet:

- **Modern cup float** (a plastic cylinder that slides up and down the fill
  valve): turn the adjustment screw on top anticlockwise, or pinch the spring
  clip on the metal rod and slide the whole cup down.
- **Older ball float** (a ball on a long arm): most have an adjustment screw
  where the arm meets the valve. If not, you can bend the arm gently downward —
  a small bend goes a long way.

Turn the water back on, let the tank fill, and check the new level. Adjust in
small increments; overshooting downward gives you a weak flush.

## If it still runs after all that

The fill valve itself is worn and no longer shutting off. You'll usually hear a
faint continuous hiss coming from the valve column rather than from the flapper
area. A universal fill valve costs about the same as two coffees, and swapping
it means turning off the water, disconnecting one supply hose, unscrewing one
plastic nut under the tank, and reversing the process. It's a genuinely easy
job — but it's a different job, and worth its own fifteen minutes.

## When to call a plumber

- Water is appearing on the **floor** rather than inside the bowl.
- The shut-off valve behind the toilet won't turn, or drips when you try it.
- The tank is cracked, or the porcelain is wet where it shouldn't be.
- The toilet rocks when you sit on it, or there is a smell at floor level.

Those are seal and mounting problems, not tank-mechanism problems, and they get
expensive if you improvise.
