Almost nobody chooses where their router goes. The engineer put a socket on
whichever wall was cheapest to reach, and the router has lived there ever since
— usually in a corner, usually on the floor, usually behind something.

That single fact explains most "slow Wi-Fi" in homes. A router radiates roughly
spherically. Put it in the corner of a rectangular house and a large share of
that sphere is going into the garden and the neighbour's wall. Move it to the
middle and the same hardware suddenly covers the place you live in.

## Placement, in order of how much it matters

**1. Central beats powerful.** The single biggest gain available to you, and it
costs nothing. Move toward the middle of the area you actually use — including
vertically. In a two-storey house, a router upstairs at the top of the stairs
often covers both floors better than one in a downstairs corner.

**2. High beats low.** Signal spreads down and outward more easily than it
climbs. Get it off the floor and onto a shelf about a metre up or higher.
Floor-level routers spend their energy on your floorboards.

**3. Open beats hidden.** Inside a TV cabinet, behind a monitor, or under a pile
of post are all meaningfully worse than out in the open. The router is not
decorative, but it is also not going to work from inside a cupboard.

**4. Away from metal and water.** A microwave, a fridge, a metal filing cabinet,
a large mirror and a fish tank are all serious obstacles. Metal reflects 2.4 GHz;
water absorbs it — which is also why a room full of people is slower than an
empty one. Give the router a metre of clearance from anything in that list.

<Callout type="tip" title="If the cable is in the wrong place">
You don't have to move the socket. A long flat Ethernet cable from the modem to
a better-placed router costs very little and solves the problem permanently.
Running one cable is nearly always cheaper and more effective than buying a
mesh system.
</Callout>

## The two bands, and why your phone lies to you

Modern routers broadcast on two frequencies at once, and understanding the
trade-off explains almost every confusing symptom.

| | 2.4 GHz | 5 GHz |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed | Lower | Much higher |
| Range | Good | Shorter |
| Through walls | Passes reasonably | Absorbed heavily |
| Congestion | Crowded — neighbours, Bluetooth, microwaves | Usually quiet |
| Best for | Far rooms, garden, smart-home gear | Same room, streaming, video calls |

Your phone shows "full bars" based on signal strength, not usable throughput.
Sitting at the far end of the house on 2.4 GHz you can have four bars and a
connection that struggles with a video call. Bars measure whether you can hear
the router; they say nothing about how much you can hear per second.

**What to actually do:** if your router presents one combined network name, leave
it — the router steers devices reasonably well. Split it into two names only
when a specific device keeps clinging to the wrong band, or when a smart plug or
camera refuses to join because it only speaks 2.4 GHz.

## Channels, briefly

In a flat or a terrace you're sharing the air with every neighbour. On 2.4 GHz
there are only three genuinely non-overlapping channels — **1, 6 and 11** — and
everyone's router is squeezed into them.

Most routers pick a channel automatically at boot and then never re-evaluate. So
a router that has been on for eight months may still be sitting on the channel
that was quiet last winter. **Restarting it forces a fresh scan**, which is a
large part of why the old "turn it off and on again" advice keeps working.

If you want to be deliberate about it, a Wi-Fi analyser app will show you which
of 1, 6 and 11 is least crowded, and you can set it manually in the router's
settings page.

## Measure properly, or you're guessing

Before and after, do the same test:

1. Stand in the **worst** room in the house — the one that prompted all this.
2. Run a speed test and write down the download, upload and ping.
3. Make one change.
4. Test again, from the same spot, holding the phone the same way.

One change at a time. If you move the router, split the bands and change the
channel all at once, you learn nothing about which of them helped.

<Callout type="warn" title="Check the line before you blame the Wi-Fi">
Plug a laptop into the router with an Ethernet cable and test again. If the wired
speed is also poor, the problem is your connection, not your Wi-Fi, and no amount
of router-shuffling will fix it — that's a call to your provider.
</Callout>

## When it is genuinely a hardware problem

Buy something only after the free changes are exhausted, and buy the right thing:

- **One room is dead, everywhere else is fine** → a single powerline adapter or
  a wired access point in that room.
- **A whole floor or wing is weak** → a mesh system. This is what mesh is for.
- **The router is more than about five years old**, or restarts on its own → it's
  the router. Older hardware also lacks the newer standards that handle many
  devices at once, which is what modern homes actually stress.

But do the free things first. A router moved two metres and lifted off the floor
has fixed more home networks than any purchase.
