A ring gets stuck for a specific reason: your finger is a cone with a bump in
the middle. The band slid on past the knuckle when the finger was smaller, and
now the knuckle is wider than the ring's inside diameter. Pulling harder does
nothing useful — it makes the finger swell more, which makes the knuckle bigger,
which makes the problem worse. That's the loop you need to break.

The string-wrap method breaks it by physically compressing the tissue in front
of the ring and then using the unwinding thread as a ramp. It is the technique
taught in first-aid training and used in emergency departments before anyone
reaches for a ring cutter.

<Callout type="stop" title="Read this before you start">
Do **not** try this — go to an urgent care clinic or emergency department now —
if the fingertip is pale, blue, grey, cold or numb; if you cannot move the
finger; if there is an injury, a possible fracture, or broken skin; if the
swelling appeared suddenly with pain, or with a rash or hives; or if the ring is
pressing into the skin. A ring that is cutting off circulation is time-critical,
and a clinic can remove it safely in under a minute.
</Callout>

## Step one is the one people skip

Everything gets easier if the finger is smaller before you start, and you can
make that happen without touching the ring at all.

**Raise the hand above heart level for five to ten minutes.** Gravity does real
work here. Sit down, rest your elbow on the back of a chair, and wait. It feels
like doing nothing; it isn't.

**Cool it at the same time.** Cold water from the tap, or a cool pack wrapped in
a cloth, constricts the small vessels and reduces swelling further. Never apply
ice directly to skin.

**Then lubricate.** Ordinary hand soap lathered thoroughly is as good as
anything. So is a water-based lubricant, hair conditioner, or petroleum jelly.
Try a gentle twist-and-pull at this point — a surprising number of rings come off
right here, and if it does, you're finished.

## The wrap

Use dental floss, thin ribbon, or elastic thread. Ordinary sewing thread is too
thin and will cut into the skin.

Start **past** the knuckle, nearer the fingertip, and wind back toward the ring
in close, even, slightly overlapping turns. Each turn should press the tissue
flat. Aim for firm and uniform — the same pressure a snug bandage gives. If the
fingertip starts to go white or throb, it's too tight: unwind, wait a minute,
and rewrap more gently.

Keep going until the wrapping meets the ring. The finger in front of the band
should now be noticeably slimmer and smooth, with the knuckle compressed flat.

## The tuck and the unwind

Take the free end you started with — the one nearest your fingertip is the wrong
one; you want the very first end, which is now buried at the ring — and slide it
**under** the band so it emerges on the palm side.

Then pull that end slowly, unwinding the wrap one turn at a time. Watch what
happens: as each turn releases, the thread under the ring lifts the band a
fraction and it advances along the compressed finger. Slow is faster here. Give
it a full minute or two if it needs one.

If the ring stops moving halfway, unwind completely, rest the hand for a few
minutes, cool it again, and rewrap. Repeating the process patiently works far
more often than pulling harder does.

<Callout type="warn" title="Two things that make it worse">
**Pulling and twisting repeatedly.** Every failed attempt inflames the tissue and
adds swelling. Give yourself two calm attempts, then stop and reassess.

**Leaving elastic on while you take a break.** Never walk away with the wrap
still in place. Unwind first.
</Callout>

## Afterwards

Wash and dry the hand, check no thread is left behind in the skin creases, and
leave the finger bare for at least a day. Some redness and a shallow groove where
the ring sat is normal and fades within a few hours.

If it happened once, it will happen again: get the ring resized, or wear it on a
chain. A jeweller can also fit a ring with a hinged shank or a sizing bar if your
knuckle is permanently larger than your finger — which is very common with age
and with arthritis, and is nothing unusual.

If pain, numbness or discolouration persists after the ring is off, get it looked
at the same day.
