Hanging a picture goes wrong for one reason: the hanger is on the **back** of
the frame and the nail goes in the **front** of the wall, and you have to
transfer a position between two things you can never see at the same time.

Everyone tries to solve this with measurement — hold the frame up, mark the top
edge, measure down from the top to the wire, measure that far below the mark.
It sounds exact. It isn't, because the wire stretches when it takes the weight,
and because you moved slightly while you were holding a heavy frame with one
hand and a pencil in the other.

The paper template removes the transfer problem entirely. You do the awkward
measuring on a table, where the frame lies flat and nothing moves.

## Making the template

Lay the frame **face-down** on a sheet of paper — newspaper, wrapping paper, a
taped-together pair of A4 sheets, anything larger than the frame. Draw around
it and cut it out. You now have a paper copy of your picture.

Now the important bit. Hook a finger under the hanging wire and **pull it up
and taut**, exactly as the hook will when the picture is on the wall. That is
where the wire will actually sit — several centimetres higher than it hangs
slack. Mark that point on the paper with a bold cross.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why this one detail decides everything">
The difference between a slack wire and a loaded wire is often 3–5 cm. Measure
the slack position and your picture hangs that much lower than you planned —
which is exactly the amount that makes it look wrong over a sofa.
</Callout>

If the frame has two D-rings or two sawtooth hangers instead of a wire, mark
both. Transferring the *spacing* between two hooks is where this method really
earns its keep.

## Getting it on the wall straight

Hold the paper cut-out against the wall and move it around. This is the step
that's genuinely enjoyable and that people skip: you can stand back, look at it
from the doorway, and try it 5 cm to the left without putting a single hole in
anything.

Some rules of thumb worth knowing while you shuffle it about:

- **On a bare wall:** centre of the picture about **145 cm (57 in)** from the
  floor. That's gallery standard and it's set at average eye level.
- **Above furniture:** forget the floor. Leave **15–25 cm** between the top of
  the sofa, console or headboard and the bottom of the frame, so they read as
  one group rather than two unrelated objects.
- **In a group:** treat the whole arrangement as one rectangle and centre
  *that*, keeping a consistent 5–8 cm gap between frames.

Once you like it, lay a spirit level along the top edge of the paper, adjust
until the bubble is dead centre, and tape all four corners down.

## Driving the fixing

Nail, screw or drill straight through the paper at your cross. The paper does
not need to be removed first — going through it is the whole point, because it
means you never transfer the mark a second time.

Pick the fixing by weight, not by habit:

| Frame weight | Into plasterboard | Into a stud or masonry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under ~2 kg | Picture hook with a pin | Picture hook with a pin |
| 2–10 kg | Plasterboard anchor rated 3× the weight | Screw into the stud |
| Over 10 kg | Two anchors, or find a stud | Two screws, or wall plugs in masonry |

<Callout type="warn" title="Check what's behind the wall first">
Electrical cables usually run vertically above and below sockets and switches,
and horizontally near ceiling level. Avoid drilling in those zones, and use a
detector if you have one. If you hit anything hard and unexpected at shallow
depth, stop and move over.
</Callout>

## Tear the paper off

Pull the template away. Your hook is sitting exactly where the wire will land,
and — a small pleasure — there are no pencil marks to rub off the wall
afterwards, because you never drew on the wall at all.

Lift the picture on, then lay the level along the **top of the frame** for a last
check. A single-wire hang can still tilt slightly if the wire is unevenly
attached; nudge one side and it settles.

## Making it stay level

Wire hangs drift. Three fixes, in order of how permanent they are:

1. **Two hooks instead of one.** The frame can no longer pivot. This is the real
   answer, and the template makes the spacing trivial.
2. **Adhesive putty** on the two bottom back corners. Grips the wall, invisible,
   removable.
3. **Self-adhesive rubber bumpers** on the bottom corners. They add friction and
   also let air circulate, which stops that grey dust outline forming on the
   wall behind the frame.

Ten minutes, one hole, straight the first time.
