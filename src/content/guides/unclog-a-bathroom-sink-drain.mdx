A bathroom sink rarely blocks all at once. It gets a little slower each week
until one morning the water is still sitting there while you brush your teeth.
That gradual decline is the clue: you are not looking at a blockage deep in the
pipework, you are looking at something growing slowly in one spot.

That spot is almost always the **tailpiece** — the short vertical pipe directly
under the plughole — and the thing growing in it is a rope of hair bound
together by soap scum. Every shampoo and every shave adds a few strands. Fatty
acids from soap react with minerals in hard water to form a sticky grey film
that glues them together. Within a year you have a felt plug wide enough to
narrow the pipe to a straw.

Here is the whole fix, from stopper to test.

## Why this works when plunging doesn't

A plunger moves water. It is excellent against a soft, mobile blockage in a
toilet, where a pressure wave can shift a mass along a wide pipe. A hair plug is
neither soft nor mobile — it is anchored to the pipe wall and to the underside
of the stopper, and it is porous, so pressure passes straight through it. You
can plunge a slow basin for ten minutes and achieve nothing but a wet floor.

The barbed tool works on the opposite principle. Its teeth are angled backwards,
so going down they fold flat and slide past the plug; coming up they open and
catch. You are not clearing a path through the hair, you are removing the hair
from the building.

<Callout type="tip" title="Get the direction of force right">
Push down slowly, pull up slowly. Almost every failed attempt comes from
shoving the tool in hard, which compacts a loose plug into a dense one and
drives it toward the trap — turning a ten-minute job into a bucket-under-the-sink
job.
</Callout>

## Getting the stopper out

Most basins use one of three stoppers, and the one you have decides how the
first minute goes.

- **Lift-and-turn.** Lift, rotate 90°, lift again. It comes free in your hand.
- **Push-button (click-clack).** Push to release, then unscrew the top
  anticlockwise. Some need a firm grip; a rubber glove gives you the friction.
- **Pop-up on a pivot rod.** The stopper is captive. Look under the basin at the
  back of the tailpiece for a horizontal rod held by a small nut. Unscrew the
  nut by hand, slide the rod out, and the stopper lifts straight up.

Photograph the pivot linkage before you disturb it. Reassembly is obvious for
about four minutes and then it isn't.

<Callout type="warn" title="If someone already poured chemicals in">
Stop. Do not put a tool or a plunger into a drain that may still hold caustic
cleaner — splashback into your eyes is a genuine risk. Let the water drain away
if it will, flush with plenty of cold water first, and wear eye protection and
gloves. If the basin is still full of treated water, a plumber is the right
call.
</Callout>

## The pull

Feed the strip down until you feel it stop, then keep going with gentle pressure
until it slips past. Twenty to thirty centimetres is usually plenty; you are
aiming to get below the plug, not to reach the drain in the street.

Then pull it up in one continuous movement. If you stop halfway and restart, the
barbs let go. What comes out is unpleasant and slightly heavier than you expect.
Wipe it straight onto a towel or into a bin bag — dropping it back in the basin
just sends it down again.

Go back in two or three more times. It is normal for the first pull to be the
dramatic one and for the third to come up nearly clean. That's your signal to
stop.

## The flush matters more than people think

Once the hair is gone there is still a layer of soap scum coating the pipe wall.
Run the **hot** tap hard for a full minute. Hot water softens the fatty film and
carries it away while the pipe is still open. Skip this and the sink will drain
fine today and start slowing again noticeably sooner.

If you want to go one better, do it with a squirt of washing-up liquid in the
basin as it drains — a degreaser doing what a degreaser is for, at a
concentration that does no harm to plastic or seals.

## When to stop and call someone

- The tool comes up clean and the sink is still slow → the trap or the branch
  waste is blocked, not the tailpiece.
- More than one fixture is slow at the same time → shared line.
- Another fixture gurgles when this one drains → a venting problem.
- Water backs up rather than merely draining slowly → stop DIY-ing now.
- There is a smell of sewage, or a damp patch appearing under the basin.

None of those are catastrophes, but they are all outside what a plastic strip
can reach.

## Keeping it clear

Two small habits keep a basin permanently fast:

1. **A hair catcher over the plughole** if the basin's design allows one. Catch
   it before it goes in and there is nothing to remove later.
2. **A ten-second pull every few months.** Run the tool down once, pull once,
   done — before you ever notice a slowdown.

That's it. No chemicals, no dismantled plumbing, and no call-out fee.
